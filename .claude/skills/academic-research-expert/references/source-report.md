# Fifteen Personas Steer a Decoupling Doctorate

The fifteen personas work as a deliberately divided expert panel built around one hard problem: showing **means–ends decoupling** in Olympic delivery governance from public documents that are themselves ceremonial "talk", before the Brisbane 2032 Games have produced any outcomes (Brisbane 2032 and an Australian AQF Level 10 university are working assumptions in every note, not verified facts). Supervisory personas own accountability, design boundaries, the argument and the candidate's wellbeing; theory personas own construct precision, causal mechanisms and the claim to a theoretical contribution; domain personas own the dated facts of contracts, budgets and audits and the content of the tourism "ends"; methodological personas own paradigm coherence, corpus construction and reflexive analysis; and compliance personas own examinability, prose and APA style, and data governance. Across the eleven note sets the strongest agreement is that the thesis must study *designed-in or prospective* decoupling up to a fixed corpus cut-off, test frontstage promises against audits, budgets and procurement records, and claim a contribution *to* institutional theory rather than an application of it *to* Brisbane. The evidence base is uneven: no published competency framework exists for any of these roles, so every persona profile is a synthesis; most regulatory and Brisbane facts were confirmed only through search-engine summaries because direct page retrieval failed; and most canonical citations come from the researchers' memory and were not re-checked. Confidence is highest for the Brisbane 2032 governance chronology, IPEd and APA currency, the Braun and Clarke corpus and the 2024–2026 compliance instruments, and lowest for verbatim regulatory text (HESF 2021 Standard 4.2, the AQF Level 10 skills wording, the 2025 National Statement exemption paragraphs), university-specific milestone and examination rules, and page-level detail in the theory canon. Two design implications follow: the panel needs written decision rights, because divergent supervisory advice unsettles candidates, and it has no designated owner for Indigenous data governance or for legal reading of the Host Contract.

## Category I: four supervisors split accountability, design, text and the person

Australian doctoral supervision sits inside four layers of rules, and the supervisory personas inherit all four. The first is the *Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research* (2018), which sets 8 principles and 29 responsibilities, R1–R13 for institutions and R14–R29 for researchers, with adherence a condition of NHMRC funding ([NHMRC](https://nhmrc.gov.au/research-policy/research-integrity/2018-australian-code-responsible-conduct-research)). Its 2019 *Supervision* guide covers institutional, supervisor and supervisee responsibilities and breaches ([ARC-hosted PDF](https://arc.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-05/Supervision-Guide-accessible.pdf)), and a WA Health training handout summarising it says supervisors remain accountable for all ethical and legal requirements and cannot hand that accountability to the student ([CAHS handout](https://cahs.health.wa.gov.au/-/media/HSPs/CAHS/Documents/Research/ResearchEducationProgram/2024-Handouts/20240719ResearchSupervisionHANDOUT), secondary). The second is the TEQSA Higher Education Standards Framework (Threshold Standards) 2021: TEQSA's guidance note (v2.1, 11 December 2024) expects research training "with adequate resources and with competent continuing supervisory arrangements" and states that the note itself imposes no obligations ([TEQSA](https://www.teqsa.gov.au/latest-news/publications/guidance-note-research-and-research-training)). The clause-level content of Standard 4.2 — a principal supervisor with a doctorate or equivalent who is active in research, plus at least one associate — is a reconstruction [U], although HELI's 2026 HDR policy mirrors that wording closely ([HELI](https://www.heli.edu.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/QAF136-Higher-Degree-Research-Policy.pdf)). The third is ACGR good practice: Principle 5 requires at least two supervisors for the whole candidature, treats supervision as a specialised academic function carrying workload, and requires active researchers ([ACGR Good Practice Principles](https://www.acgr.edu.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/ACGR_Good-Practice-Principles.pdf)). The fourth is university procedure, where the usual pattern of confirmation at about 12 months full-time equivalent, mid-candidature at 18–24 months and pre-submission at 30–36 months, inside a Research Training Program funding horizon of up to four years, is [C/U] and was not verified for UQ, Griffith, QUT or Monash.

**"Doctoral Research Chair" is not an Australian title.** Australian policy uses principal (or primary) and associate (or co-) supervisors, milestone panels have a panel chair, and "committee chair" is North American usage, so the notes model both Chair personas as the senior principal supervisor's academic-leadership and gatekeeping functions and the General Supervisors as the team's relational functions [I]. They also recommend that every persona output separate "must" (HESF, the Code), "should" (ACGR, UKCGE) and "local" (university procedure) [I].

The supervision "models" these personas draw on are typologies grounded in supervisor interviews rather than interventions tested against outcomes. Lee's (2008) five concepts are functional, enculturation, critical thinking, emancipation and developing a quality relationship ([UKCGE abstract](https://supervision.ukcge.ac.uk/resources/resource-library/how-are-doctoral-students-supervised-concepts-of-doctoral-research-supervision)); Gatfield's (2005) structure-by-support grid (laissez-faire, pastoral, contractual, directorial) was checked against 12 supervisor interviews and is "dynamic" across candidature ([UKCGE abstract](https://supervision.ukcge.ac.uk/resources/resource-library/an-investigation-into-phd-supervisory-management-styles-development-of-a-dynamic-conceptual-model-and-its-managerial-implications)); and Halse and Malfroy (2010) describe five facets — learning alliance, habits of mind, scholarly expertise, techne and contextual expertise ([WSU portal](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/retheorizing-doctoral-supervision-as-professional-work/)). The notes treat all three as diagnostic lenses, not prescriptions [I]. The UKCGE Good Supervisory Practice Framework, written by Stan Taylor and reorganised into 10 domains after trials in 13 institutions ([Taylor, UKCGE slides](https://ukcge.ac.uk/assets/pdfs/ac22-Stan-Taylor-Uses-of-the-framework.pdf)), underpins a recognition scheme requiring a reflective account of about 5,000 words ([UKCGE](https://supervision.ukcge.ac.uk/good-supervisory-practice-framework)); four of the ten domain names are unconfirmed [U]. No meta-analysis comparing supervision styles on completion or quality outcomes was found.

### Chair 1 owns theory, findings and the claim to doctorateness

The first Chair acts as the senior principal supervisor who carries the non-delegable duties — integrity accountability, milestone sign-off, certification for submission and examiner nomination — and owns the claim that the thesis meets AQF Level 10 [I]. Working mostly in Lee's enculturation and critical-thinking modes [I], this persona intervenes at fixed points. At confirmation it tests whether means–ends decoupling, rather than policy–practice decoupling or loose coupling, is the right lens; at mid-candidature it applies a "conceptual conclusions" test, asking whether each findings chapter says something about decoupling or leverage *as concepts* beyond what happened in the case, drawing on Trafford and Leshem's treatment of doctorateness as a threshold concept (2008, 2009) [C]; and at pre-submission it reads abstract, introduction and conclusion first, as Mullins and Kiley's (2002) experienced examiners do [C], before nominating examiners who span institutional theory and event, tourism or legacy studies [I]. Its working artefacts are a one-page alignment matrix shared with Chair 2 (problem, research question, construct, documentary evidence, procedure and contribution type), a one-paragraph contribution claim revised every six months, milestone reports, an examiner-nomination rationale and margin notes that ask "so what for theory?" [I]. No scholarly source for the "golden thread" metaphor was found and it appears to be practitioner vocabulary [U]; the scholarly anchors are Ravitch and Riggan's idea of a conceptual framework as an argument (a third edition is unverified) and Maxwell's (2013) interactive design model with the research questions at its hub [C].

Chair 1's diagnostic questions follow the thesis's central risk [I]: what is decoupled from what, for which organisation (IOC, Commonwealth, Queensland, organising committee or delivery authority, councils) and in which period; whether the evidence concerns talk (strategies, legacy plans) or action (budgets, procurement, audits) in Brunsson's sense; whether "legacy" is used analytically, following Preuss, or normatively; whether ex-ante leverage (Chalip) is kept apart from ex-post legacy; and whether the finding would surprise an institutional theorist or only an events scholar. The skills behind those questions are theoretical judgement about when a concept does analytic work and when it is decoration, translation between organisation sociology, policy studies and tourism, knowledge of the examiner market, strategic patience while the candidate crosses theory thresholds (Kiley and Wisker 2009 [C]), and arbitration within the team [I]. The knowledge base combines the neo-institutional canon — Meyer and Rowan (1977), DiMaggio and Powell (1983) and Brunsson (1989) [C], plus Bromley and Powell (2012) ([Taylor & Francis record](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/19416520.2012.684462)) and Wijen (2014) ([EUR repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/51762)) — with mega-event and legacy work (Preuss 2007, Chalip 2004, Müller 2015, Flyvbjerg, Budzier and Lunn 2021, Thomson et al. 2019 [C]), doctorateness research (Trafford and Leshem; Mullins and Kiley 2002; Wellington 2013 [C]), and the supervision typologies and regulatory layers above.

The red flags Chair 1 catches are theory introduced in chapter 2 and absent from the analysis, decoupling asserted from rhetoric alone, legacy left undefined or normative, findings written as chronologies, a contribution phrased as "fills a gap", causal over-claiming from documents, neglect of Paralympic and First Nations dimensions, and scope inflated to "all mega-events" [I]. Chair 1 defers to Chair 2 on feasibility, briefs the editing supervisor on the overarching argument, draws on the institutional sociologist and tourism strategist for depth, and anticipates the committee reviewer [I]. **Confidence: moderate.** The formal remit rests on verified but summary-level regulatory sources, while the behaviours are synthesis that no study validates as a functional split.

### Chair 2 owns the bridge from theory to a bounded, cut-off corpus

The second Chair is a co-principal or senior associate supervisor, or a milestone-panel chair, accountable for design coherence, feasibility within the four-year funding horizon [C] and supervisory oversight of case and corpus boundaries — the detailed case-study method sits with the methodologist — while making sure data-management and integrity duties are designed in from the start [I]. It works in Lee's functional and critical-thinking modes and Gatfield's contractual style, and the notes expect structure to be high at confirmation, ease as the candidate becomes independent, and rise again before submission [I].

Chair 2 co-owns the alignment matrix and keeps a **boundary decision log** that records each scoping decision (time window, organisations in or out, document types, cut-off date) against the research question that justifies it [I]. Before confirmation it requires a corpus protocol (sources, search strings, RTI requests, inclusion and exclusion criteria, version control) and a data management plan, and at mid-candidature it audits the balance between "talk" and "action" documents [I]. The supervisory notes call this a "saturation" audit, but the methodologist and RTA notes reject saturation for this design, so this report recommends framing it as **corpus adequacy** [I]. Its diagnostic questions ask for the case's subject and object in Thomas's (2011) sense [C], its temporal bounds and the corpus cut-off while the Games are still being delivered, whether the design is an embedded single case or several comparator Games, how gaps in the public record are handled through RTI, estimates hearings and audits, and whether each boundary traces back to a research question [I]. The design risk Chair 2 must own is that official documents are where organisations stage ceremonial conformity, so a documents-only corpus over-samples talk; means–ends decoupling needs audits, evaluations, budget papers and procurement records [I]. The governance notes show why the cut-off matters: Brisbane's venue plan moved from the 2021 bid to a February 2023 Gabba rebuild, a March 2024 QSAC plan and a March 2025 Victoria Park plan (see Persona 7).

Chair 2 also carries the generative-AI duties. TEQSA's *Gen AI strategies for research training: Emerging practice* (26 June 2025) asks institutions to give all supervision staff clear direction, to consider a formal gen-AI use agreement signed by candidate and supervisors at commencement, and to build discipline-specific AI literacy covering evaluation of outputs, data integrity, IP and attribution of authorship ([TEQSA toolkit](https://www.teqsa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-06/Gen-AI-strategies-research-training-emerging-practice-toolkit.pdf)). The supervisory notes found no ACGR statement, but the editing notes verified ACGR's *Good Practice Guidelines for Generative Artificial Intelligence Use in Graduate Research Training* (December 2024), which require use to be "responsible, ethical, and consistent with the core principles of research integrity" ([ACGR](https://acgr.edu.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/ACGR-Good-Practice-Guidelines-for-Generative-Artificial-Intelligence-Use-in-Graduate-Research-Training.pdf)). Chair 2 therefore co-signs an AI-use agreement, reviews it at each milestone, and bars uploading RTI-released, commercial-in-confidence or First Nations material to third-party tools [I].

The knowledge base is design-oriented: Maxwell (2013), Ravitch and Riggan (2017), Yin (2018), Stake (1995), Thomas (2011), Ragin and Becker (1992), Baxter and Jack (2008), Flyvbjerg (2006), Bowen (2009) and Lincoln and Guba (1985) [C], together with the Code's data guide, HESF 4.2 and the TEQSA toolkit. Its skills are design thinking (seeing how a change in one component ripples through the others), project and risk management, pragmatic judgement about feasibility and document access, and translating abstract constructs into document-observable indicators without positivist operationalisation [I]. It watches for a boundary that drifts as news breaks, a missing cut-off, a corpus of public-relations documents, research questions the data cannot answer, a "multiple case" claim without comparative logic, a methods chapter that describes a different study, a missing audit trail, AI tools used on confidential documents, and ignored RTI delays [I]. **Confidence: moderate**, because the design logic rests on canonical but unchecked case-study texts and the university milestone rules are unverified.

### The editing supervisor owns the text as argument, not the copyedit

Research on supervisors' written feedback converges on four points: feedback serves referential, directive and expressive functions; it socialises candidates into a discourse community; it is emotionally loaded; and writing is research practice rather than a final "write-up". Kumar and Stracke (2007) coded feedback on a first full draft by those three speech functions and found expressive feedback most beneficial, and a 2019 follow-up using their model found advisers taking evaluator, copy-editor or co-author roles ([University of Canberra](https://researchprofiles.canberra.edu.au/en/publications/an-analysis-of-written-feedback-on-a-phd-thesis/)). Basturkmen, East and Bitchener (2014) frame on-script comments as socialisation into the academic discourse community ([search record](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/aral.39.2.01bit)). Carter and Kumar, drawing on 11 interviews and 226 questionnaires, found that completion pressure can crowd out writing development and that feedback must balance honesty against discouragement ([UKCGE](https://supervision.ukcge.ac.uk/resources/resource-library/ignoring-me-is-part-of-learning-supervisory-feedback-on-doctoral-writing)); sources date the paper to 2016 or 2017 [U]. Carter, Guerin and Aitchison's *Doctoral Writing* (2020) grew out of the DoctoralWriting SIG blog, which had more than 14,800 followers in October 2019 ([Springer](https://www.springer.com/gp/book/9789811518072)), and Kamler and Thomson's *Helping Doctoral Students Write* is current in its 2014 second edition ([Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/Helping-Doctoral-Students-Write-Pedagogies-for-supervision/Kamler-Thomson/p/book/9780415823494)), with its framing of writing as text work and identity work and its four-move "tiny text" abstract (locate, focus, report, argue) given as [C].

The decisive boundary comes from IPEd's *Guidelines for editing theses* (July 2025), which make supervisors responsible for editorial advice on content, structure and language while restricting professional editors to copyediting and proofreading ([IPEd 2025](https://www.iped-editors.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/IPEd_guidelines_for_editing_theses_July_2025-1.pdf)). **Developmental editing therefore belongs to this supervisory persona, not to the two Category IV editing personas** [I]. Its routine triages feedback by level (argument and structure before sentences), reverse-outlines each chapter, Toulmin-maps the three to five central claims of each findings chapter (claim, grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal [C]), asks for a four-move tiny text before line editing, balances directive, referential and expressive comments, requires a response-to-feedback table (comment, action, rationale) that builds feedback literacy (Carless and Boud 2018 [C]) and an audit trail, and agrees turnaround norms [I]. The artefacts are annotated drafts with a cover note listing the top three priorities, a chapter argument map, a terminology glossary (legacy, leverage, impact, decoupling, loose coupling, legitimacy), a citation audit and a pre-submission consistency checklist; typical questions are "What is this chapter's claim in one sentence?", "Where does the reader first meet decoupling in your data?" and "Is this paragraph reporting the literature or using it?" [I].

The skills are diagnostic reading at several levels, clear, kind and specific feedback, genre knowledge of social-science and tourism theses (Swales's CARS model [C]; Paltridge and Starfield's macro-structures), restraint against rewriting — because a "co-author" role raises questions about who authored the thesis — managing the emotional load of feedback, and spotting patchwriting or AI-generated passages [I]. The red flags are "he said, she said" literature reviews, absent authorial voice, data-dump findings chapters, an argument that does not accumulate across chapters, drifting key terms, new material in the conclusion, an abstract misaligned with the conclusion, fabricated references, sudden style shifts that suggest undisclosed AI or third-party writing, and over-dependence on the supervisor's line edits [I]. The editing supervisor takes the macro argument from Chair 1 and the structure from Chair 2, hands copyediting to the Doctoral Writing Specialist within IPEd limits, refers writing anxiety to the General Supervisors, and keeps supervisor, professional-editor and AI input separate and acknowledged [I]. **Confidence: moderate to high** for the feedback research, several items of which were verified at abstract level, and **moderate** for the routines, which are practitioner techniques (no empirical evaluation of reverse outlining was found [U]).

### General supervisors own process integrity and the candidate as a person

Peer debriefing comes from Lincoln and Guba's (1985) credibility techniques, in which a disinterested peer keeps the inquirer honest, tests working hypotheses, develops next steps and provides catharsis [C]. Spall (1998) found debriefing works best with trust, a methodology focus and continuing skill development, often in pairs or small groups ([KB record](https://ibl.kb.nl/articles/0000000110326/4/2)); Olmos-Vega et al.'s AMEE Guide No. 149 (2023) defines reflexivity as ongoing, collaborative and multifaceted and warns that it is often handled superficially ([McMaster Experts](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/2182482)); and Smith and McGannon (2018) recommend critical friends who challenge interpretations rather than seek consensus ([Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1323112)). The notes' key inference is that **supervisors are not disinterested peers** — they hold assessment power and intellectual investment — so this persona runs supervisory debriefs on interpretation and method while pushing debriefing outside the hierarchy through writing groups, HDR panels or an outside critical friend [I].

For a documents-only design the routines are a debrief after each analysis cycle that asks which documents surprised the candidate, where decoupling was *read in* rather than found, what a disconfirming document would look like and whose voice is missing from the archive; a positionality statement drafted at confirmation and revised at later milestones, covering the candidate's relationship to Brisbane, any tourism or government employment and normative views on mega-events; a candidate-controlled reflexive journal; and spot checks of the corpus audit trail [I]. Because subjectivity enters a document study through which documents are found or released, through the interpretive frame and through the theory's pull toward seeing decoupling everywhere, the prompts target confirmation bias as well as personal background [I].

The pastoral evidence is the best-verified empirical material in Category I. Levecque et al. (2017) found **32% of 3,659 Flemish PhD students at risk of a common psychiatric disorder** on the GHQ-12 — a screening-based risk, not a diagnosis rate — with the supervisor's leadership style among the correlates ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/respol/v46y2017i4p868-879.html)); Hazell et al. (2020) found doctoral researchers report significantly higher stress than population norms across 52 studies ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7450565)); and Satinsky et al. (2021) pooled depression prevalence at **0.24 (95% CI 0.18–0.31)** across 32 articles ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8277873/)). Evans et al.'s (2018) widely quoted figures could not be verified and the notes advise against citing them [U]. ACGR's mental health and wellbeing guidelines (2021) call for an integrated institutional approach focused on modifiable factors ([ACGR](https://www.acgr.edu.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/ACGR_GPG_MentalHealth.pdf)), its 2026 webinar "Supervisors Who Support" introduced "wellbeing literacy" for supervisors ([ACGR event](https://www.acgr.edu.au/event/acgr-webinar-1-2026-supervisors-who-support/)), and Guerin and Green (2015) found candidates experience differing supervisor opinions as "confusing and unsettling" and seek consensus ([UKCGE](https://supervision.ukcge.ac.uk/resources/resource-library/theyre-the-bosses-feedback-in-team-supervision)).

The pastoral routines are an expectations conversation at commencement revisited after each milestone, a brief wellbeing check-in kept separate from progress talk, "recognise, respond, refer" to counselling, the graduate school, disability services or crisis lines (the crisis-line numbers in the notes are [C] and must be confirmed), meeting notes with an agreed action log, tempo management around confirmation, document-access delays, stipend end dates and visa conditions, and an escalation ladder from the team through the HDR coordinator, Associate Dean and graduate school to ombudsman routes [I]. The boundaries are to avoid dual relationships, not to diagnose, to keep informal supervision bounded (Hemer 2012 [C]), and to remember that distress is disclosed across a power gap [I]. Because supervisor leadership style is a modifiable correlate of risk, Gatfield's low-structure, low-support laissez-faire quadrant is a pastoral risk and not just a teaching choice [I]. Where documents concern First Nations land, culture or participation, this persona prompts engagement with the AIATSIS Code (2020) and Indigenous data sovereignty principles even though the study has no participants [C/I].

Its skills are active listening, building psychological safety, mental-health literacy with knowledge of role limits, facilitating reflexive dialogue, de-escalating conflict, cultural humility (Manathunga 2014 [C]), disciplined record-keeping and awareness of its own power [I]. It watches for Manathunga's (2005) early warning signs — missed meetings, avoidance, repeated topic changes, no written output, isolation [C] — as well as perfectionism loops, distress, a candidate caught between conflicting supervisors, unacknowledged insider positions, debriefing that turns into rubber-stamping, bullying or boundary violations, and stipend, visa or caring pressures [I]. Several 2025–2026 developments could not be verified, including a National Student Ombudsman and a National Higher Education Code on gender-based violence with staff–student relationship provisions [U], and the content of ACGR's *Principles for Respectful Supervisory Relationships* was not retrieved. **Confidence: moderate to high** for the wellbeing evidence, **moderate** for debriefing practice, and **low** for 2023–2026 Australian HDR prevalence data and new regulatory duties.

### A written decision-rights rule keeps four supervisors from confusing one candidate

Because the four-persona panel is itself a team-supervision design, and Guerin and Green's finding shows divergent advice unsettles candidates, the notes propose explicit decision rights [I]. Chair 1 decides theory and contribution; Chair 2 decides scope, design and feasibility; the editing supervisor decides textual presentation; and the General Supervisors can call a pause on wellbeing grounds. Disagreements are written down and resolved in a team meeting rather than passed to the candidate unresolved. Confirmation is owned by both Chairs, mid-candidature is led by Chair 2 with Chair 1 reviewing findings, and pre-submission is led by Chair 1, with the editing supervisor auditing the text and the General Supervisors confirming the audit trail is complete. No empirical study validates this split; team-supervision research mostly describes informal role division. **Overall confidence for Category I: moderate.**

## Category II: theorists police constructs while domain experts anchor dates and ends

### The institutional sociologist demands "decoupling of what from what, for whom"

This persona is the panel's guardian of theory. It makes sure neo-institutional theory, and means–ends decoupling in particular, is used precisely and in line with the field's genealogy, that decoupling is made observable in documents in a defensible way, and that the thesis claims a contribution *to* institutional theory rather than only applying it *to* Brisbane [I]. It works by Socratic interrogation of the link between theory and data, captured in one question: "decoupling of what from what, for whom, under what pressure, and how would you know?" [I].

The genealogy it expects a candidate to command runs from the 1977 founding papers of Meyer and Rowan and of Zucker, through isomorphism (DiMaggio and Powell 1983), diffusion (Tolbert and Zucker 1983), legitimacy (Suchman 1995), logics (Thornton, Ocasio and Lounsbury 2012), institutional work (Lawrence and Suddaby 2006), complexity (Greenwood et al. 2011) and micro-foundations (Powell and Colyvas 2008) [C]. The core debate is verified at record or abstract level. The persona's operational scheme is a **commitment chain** [I]. For each stated end E, such as "a lasting visitor-economy legacy for regional Queensland", the candidate records its institutional source, the stated means M, implementation traces I (procurement notices, budget papers, annual reports, minutes, RTI releases, Hansard), outcome provisions O and the causal theory C linking M to E. A chain shows policy–practice decoupling when M or E is stated but I is absent; means–ends decoupling when I is present and compliant but O measures outputs or proxies, C is missing or implausible given the opacity, or responsibility for E falls outside any continuing organisation; coupling when M, I and O align with feedback; and selective coupling when some components are coupled and others sit in a legacy unit without decision rights. Ceremonial indicators include boilerplate lifted from IOC templates, aspirational modality without owners, budgets or dates, output-only KPIs, no baseline, evaluation deferred past the Games, legacy bodies without budget authority, unexplained target drift, and commitments that appear only in frontstage genres; substantive coupling shows up as budget lines and contract clauses, baselined targets, owners whose role extends past 2032, independent assurance with consequences, and documented revision after evaluation. Document versions are ordered in time (2021 candidature → Host Contract → 100 Day Review in 2025 → 2032 Delivery Plan) and coded for triggers and the direction of change. These constructs work as sensitising and pattern-matching devices (Blumer; Reay and Jones 2016 [C]) rather than a closed codebook, and because documents are themselves ceremonial artefacts, claims are phrased as "documentary evidence of decoupling". If the corpus ends before 2032, the thesis studies **designed-in or prospective decoupling** and must say so as a scope condition [I].

The mechanism hypotheses for Brisbane are goal proliferation across IOC, Commonwealth, State, council and First Nations commitments; causal opacity in tourism ends; measurement proxies; temporary organisations that dissolve before ends mature; and audits and reviews that either recouple to ends or tighten coupling to budget-and-time proxies [I]. The persona keeps apart recoupling to ends, tight coupling to proxies (Sauder and Espeland 2009 [C]) and re-legitimation without changed practice [I]. Organisational behaviour adds a distinction it polices hard: Weick's loose coupling (1976; Orton and Weick 1990 [C]) is a structural property, not legitimacy-driven decoupling, and mega-event delivery networks are loosely coupled by design [I]. Sensegiving and identity in ministerial statements and review reports can be studied through documents, whereas tacit everyday practice cannot [I].

The persona's habitual questions ask the candidate to state the decoupling claim in one sentence, name its form and say why not the others, point to the document, clause and date for each end, identify the legitimating audience with evidence independent of the adoption, show a coupled chain for contrast, rule out rival explanations, and say what a temporary, multi-level, pre-event setting teaches institutional theory that firm-level CSR studies cannot [I]. The red flags are treating any gap as decoupling without first ruling out incapacity, shocks, cost escalation or deliberate re-prioritisation; tautology; conflating decoupling with greenwashing; judging means against ends the researcher prefers; reading talk as practice; ignoring power; assuming coupling is always good; slipping between field, organisation and programme levels; treating decoupling as a state rather than a process; edition errors such as citing Scott's 3rd edition as current or the 2008 handbook chapters for their 2017 versions; relying on a single definitional source; and claiming to "test" decoupling theory with one pre-event case [I]. Its safeguards are a construct glossary, a **decoupling claim protocol** (pressure → audience → adoption → separation → rivals ruled out), a negative-case search and reflexive notes on the candidate's stance toward the Olympics [I]. Its skills are command of both the sociology and management literatures, Sartori's ladder of abstraction, typology building, abductive and processual theorising, critical reading of accountability instruments, examiner anticipation and journal targeting (*Organization Studies*, *JMS*, *JMI*, *Sport Management Review*, *IJSPP*, *Annals of Tourism Research*) [I]. Its candidate contributions are means–ends decoupling in temporary, multi-organisation delivery systems (no source theorises this, though Lundin and Söderholm 1995 on temporary organisations is a possible link [C]), promissory pre-event decoupling, and formal reviews as triggers for recoupling or re-legitimation [I]. **Confidence: moderate to high** for the 2012–2025 debate, **moderate** for the canon, where definitions must be quoted from the originals, and **low** for the claim that no Olympic decoupling study exists.

### Theory-fusion theorists audit commensurability and specify mechanisms

The Senior Academic Theorists are the thesis's theoretical architects. They decide which public-management and sociological theories may be combined, audit each theory's assumptions for compatibility, name bridging constructs, specify the mechanisms linking governance structures to decoupled outcomes, and hold the claimed contribution to Level 10 originality [I]. Their habits come from the craft-of-theorising literature: abduction and problematisation rather than gap-spotting, an explicit form of theory rather than a boilerplate "conceptual framework", and stated scope conditions [C/I]. This researcher completed only four searches. The verified anchors are Oswick, Fleming and Hanlon (2011), who argue organisation theory's one-way borrowing yields few original ideas and propose two-way conceptual blending ([City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/13984/)); Okhuysen and Bonardi (2011), verified only as a bibliographic record, whose recalled test judges a combination by the proximity of the phenomena and the compatibility of the assumptions ([Serval](https://serval.unil.ch/notice/serval:BIB_7884E13DB5F5); content [U]); Cornelissen, Höllerer and Seidl (2021), who map propositional, process, configurational, perspectival and meta-theorising forms and warn that equating theory with mechanisms is only one view ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/26317877211020328)); and Polzer et al. (2016), who read public-administration paradigms as institutional logics (bureaucracy, market, democracy), distinguish layering from blending, and found layering in parliamentary debates on Austrian budget-law reforms — a document-based precedent close to this thesis ([Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/S0733-558X201600048B003)). Everything else in this persona's base is [C].

The persona treats traditional public administration, New Public Management, New Public Governance, public value and post-NPM reform as **layered, coexisting repertoires, not eras** [I]. The [C] canon behind this includes Hood (1991) on NPM's seven doctrines and their roots in new institutional economics plus managerialism; Bevan and Hood (2006) on target gaming through ratchet, threshold and output-distortion effects; Osborne (2006), who roots NPG in institutional and network theory — the main justification for fusing it with organisational sociology; Moore's (1995) strategic triangle and Rhodes and Wanna's (2007) Australian critique of public value; Goodwin and Grix (2011) on "asymmetrical network governance" in sport policy; Provan and Kenis (2008); Sørensen and Torfing (2009) on metagovernance; Ansell and Gash (2008) and Emerson, Nabatchi and Balogh (2012) on collaborative governance; Bovens (2007) on accountability as an actor–forum relationship; Koppell (2005) on "multiple accountabilities disorder" in hybrid bodies; Pollitt et al. (2004), van Thiel (2004) and Flinders and Buller (2006) on agencies and depoliticisation; Christensen et al.'s instrument–culture–myth model; Pollitt's (2001) four stages of convergence (discursive, decisional, practice, results), described as **the most direct public-administration counterpart to decoupling**; and Hall and Taylor (1996) on three institutionalisms. For Brisbane, the persona expects a hybrid of NPM-style disaggregated statutory authorities inside Commonwealth–State–Council agreements under IOC contractual metagovernance [I].

Mechanism-based explanation draws on four families [C]: analytical sociology (Hedström and Swedberg; Hedström and Ylikoski 2010; Coleman's macro–micro–macro model); pragmatist and field accounts (Gross 2009; Davis and Marquis 2005); context-sensitive political science (Falleti and Lynch 2009; Beach and Pedersen's process tracing; Collier's 2011 hoop and smoking-gun tests); and critical realism with historical institutionalism (Bhaskar, Archer, Sayer, Pierson, Capoccia and Kelemen 2007, and Mahoney and Thelen's 2010 modes of displacement, layering, drift and conversion), joined by Barley and Tolbert's (1997) structuration bridge. The recommended stance is critical realism as meta-theory, historical institutionalism to sequence the bid, award, delivery and post-Games phases, and institutional and public-administration theory as the substantive content [I]. Six candidate mechanisms follow, each a hypothesis to trace rather than a finding [I]: output-contract substitution, where venues on time and on budget stand in for legacy ends; temporal compression and sunset, where deadlines and time-limited bodies let delivery dominate and legacy drift after wind-up; depoliticised accountability shift toward managerial and financial forums that assess means, not ends; metagovernance-induced ceremonial conformity to IOC requirements; layering of legacy institutions onto a delivery logic; and network diffusion of responsibility that leaves "orphaned ends". Falleti and Lynch imply that the thesis must say which Australian features — federal fiscal relations, Westminster ministerial responsibility, statutory authorities, electoral cycles — are part of the mechanism rather than background [I].

The persona judges fusing institutional theory with NPM to be the hardest pairing for commensurability, because public choice and principal–agent theory assume calculating actors while sociological institutionalism assumes appropriateness, whereas NPG, network governance and historical institutionalism fuse more easily [I]. Four justifications recur — ontological mapping (Polzer), shared ancestry in Simon, March and Selznick, a division of explanatory labour by level or time (Poole and Van de Ven), and a meta-theoretical umbrella such as critical realism or structuration — and the notes favour ancestry plus a critical-realist umbrella [I]. The combination protocol states the explanandum, lists each source theory's assumptions, names and defines bridging constructs (legitimacy, performance regime, accountability forum, structural disaggregation) in Suddaby's terms, chooses the mode of combination and the form of theory, states boundary conditions, specifies documentary traces, and says what changes in each parent theory [I]. The persona's **contribution test** has seven items: a theory delta ("before this thesis, theory said X; now we know Y"); at least one mechanism specified with entities, activities and conditions; boundary conditions that are themselves theorised; portability to Expos, Commonwealth Games, city deals, megaprojects and disaster-recovery authorities; two-way blending that returns insights to both parent theories; a problematised assumption, such as decoupling theory's implicit stable, permanent organisation; and a recognisable artefact (process model, typology or propositions) [I]. "Application only" looks like theories listed side by side and then used as labels ("this is NPM", "this is decoupling") [I]. The candidate contributions are temporal means–ends decoupling in time-bound special-purpose governance, nested metagovernance, a typology of legacy-governance arrangements crossing structural separation, accountability forum and legacy ownership, and a process model of legacy commitments moving through layering, drift and conversion [I]. Sport and mega-event research mostly applies one borrowed theory at a time without commensurability arguments, which is a plausible opening that no systematic search has confirmed [I].

The skills are assumption auditing, construct definition, mechanism specification, choice of theory form, boundary-setting, and fluency in both the public-administration journals (*PAR*, *PMR*, *JPART*, *Public Administration*) and the sociology and organisation-theory journals (*AMR*, *OS*, *JMS*, *ASR*, *Sociological Theory*) [I]. The red flags are "decoupling" used for any plan–outcome gap, paradigms treated as eras ("Brisbane 2032 is NPG"), six or more theories with no integration section, mechanisms inferred from strategy documents alone, teleological legacy narratives, legacy outcome claims before the Games, mega-event exceptionalism without theorised conditions, untheorised Australian context, and a "new paradigm" overclaim (Hambrick 2007 [C]) when Australian examiners expect a bounded contribution (Mullins and Kiley 2002 [C]) [I]. **Confidence: low to moderate** on verification; the works are heavily cited, so the main risk is detail error rather than non-existent sources.

The red flags are treating promises as outcomes; writing that "the IOC requires Australia to…"; blaming the organising committee for venue overruns that GIICA now owns; treating Coordination Commission praise as assurance; the "envelope equals cost" fallacy; describing the Gabba rebuild, QSAC or the Arena as current; analysing a moving project as if frozen; quoting limited-assurance conclusions as definitive; review naivety; calling every overrun decoupling; relying on IOC self-reporting; laundering media figures; and ignoring the federal layer and rule-of-exception law [I]. **Confidence: high** for the Brisbane chronology and audit findings, which are dated and multiply sourced though partly through news; **moderate** for the IOC architecture; and **low** for Host Contract clause content — the IOC's financial contribution, surplus sharing, termination triggers and dispute clause were never retrieved [U] — while the GIICA chair, CEO and responsible minister are recalled rather than verified [U].

### Tourism strategists reconcile misaligned targets before testing any finding

The Tourism Legacy and Leverage Strategist works like a visitor-economy policy evaluator with event-economics literacy, and its organising distinction is that **legacy is an ex-post state judged against a counterfactual, while leverage is an ex-ante plan with means** [C/I]. A strategy that lists legacy aspirations without leverage means (owners, budgets, programmes, KPIs, timing) is exactly the configuration institutional theory reads as means–ends decoupling, and Smith's (2014) critique of leverage as "convenient justification" is the tourism-studies analogue of ceremonial conformity [C/I]. The canon is all [C]: Preuss's (2007) legacy definition and "legacy cube" (planned/unplanned, positive/negative, tangible/intangible), Gratton and Preuss (2008) on net legacy, Preuss (2015) on changes to a host's "location factors" and Preuss (2019) on measurement against a counterfactual and baseline; Preuss's (2005) visitor typology, which distinguishes home-stayers, runaways and changers among residents from event visitors, casuals, time-switchers, extentioners and avoiders among non-residents; Chalip's (2004) general leverage model built on two resources (event visitors and trade; event media), Chalip (2006) on social leverage through liminality and communitas, and O'Brien (2006) on Sydney 2000's Business Club Australia; Ziakas on event portfolios; Kaplanidou and Vogt (2007) on event and destination image; Sant, Mason and Hinch (2013) on destination marketing organisations; Thomson et al. (2019), from a largely Griffith University team; and Byers et al. (2020) on legacy as a wicked problem. Two developments are verified: the IOC replaced its 12-year, 126-indicator Olympic Games Impact (OGI) study with a host-led legacy reporting framework from Tokyo 2020 onward ([IOC](https://olympics.com/ioc/news/tokyo-2020-to-pioneer-new-legacy-report)), and the OECD published an independent *Legacy of the Paris 2024 Olympic and Paralympic Games* in September 2025 ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/09/the-legacy-of-the-paris-2024-olympic-and-paralympic-games_fa7c7102/full-report/iconic-games-opening-the-city-for-greater-engagement-in-sports-and-culture_b80ea5ff.html)). The persona reads the end of the OGI as a channel through which ceremonial legacy accounts can be produced without standardised outcome indicators [I].

Ex-post evidence shows Games-time tourism is often flat or displaced. UK visits fell **4.2%** year on year in July–September 2012 while earnings rose **8.5%** ([ONS Travel Trends](https://doc.ukdataservice.ac.uk/doc/7087/mrdoc/pdf/7087_travel_trends_2012.pdf)), and about 680,000 Games-related visits averaged £1,350 in spend ([VisitBritain release](https://travelprnews.com/visitbritain-strong-september-figures-signal-post-olympics-bounce-29581/travel-press-release/2012/11/15/amp/)), against pre-Games messaging of "at least a million extra people". Paris 2024 drew about 11.2 million visitors, including 3.1 million tourists (+19.2% on 2023) ([Paris je t'aime](https://parisjetaime.com/eng/media/article/preliminary-report-olympic-games-a1755)); visitors were about 85% French, and the Louvre, Musée d'Orsay and Versailles were down 22.2%, 29% and 25% ([Sports Examiner](https://www.thesportsexaminer.com/paris-2024-follow-up-report-shows-paris-2024-olympic-visitors-were-85-french-up-420000-tourists-during-the-games-period-over-2023/)). The OECD reports 7.1 million tourist stays (+11%) and a 2023 survey in which nearly half of Île-de-France residents viewed the Games unfavourably, and full-year Paris Region visitors rose only about 2% ([Tourism Review](https://www.tourism-review.com/number-of-international-visitors-did-not-grow-much-in-paris-news14865)). At Gold Coast 2018, domestic overnight visitors spent **about half** the pre-Games estimate but travelled further into Queensland than expected ([GC2018 economic impacts report](https://library.olympics.com/fiba/digitalCollection/DigitalCollectionAttachmentDownloadHandler.ashx?parentDocumentId=3023688&documentId=3023689&skipWatermark=true&skipCopyright=true)). Higher spend per visitor can coexist with fewer visits, so every legacy claim must name its metric — visits, nights, spend, GSP or welfare [I]. Weed (2008), Solberg and Preuss (2007), Vierhaus (2019) and Rose and Spiegel's (2011) bid-signal effect are [C], while Fourie and Santana-Gallego's (2011) recalled "about 8%" arrivals effect and the A$2.1bn net consumption loss often attributed to Giesecke and Madden's (2011) Sydney retrospective are unverified.

On methods, input–output multipliers measure gross activity and overstate benefits, CGE models net economy-wide effects, and cost–benefit analysis asks whether residents are better off (Dwyer, Forsyth and Spurr 2004–2006; Crompton 1995 on eleven misapplications and 2006 on commissioned studies as "political shenanigans"; Késenne 2005; Taks et al. 2011 [C]). Paris shows the gap between ex-ante and audit evidence: CDES's May 2024 update projected a €6.7–11.1bn net gain with an intermediate €8.9bn ([Francs Jeux](https://www.francsjeux.com/en/2024/05/14/Paris-2024-an-economic-impact-of-9-billion-euros/100873/)), whereas the Cour des comptes judged the impact "limited", estimated a 2024 growth effect of about €1.9bn and warned that organiser-financed studies tend to overestimate ([Banque des Territoires](https://www.banquedesterritoires.fr/pour-la-cour-des-comptes-paris-2024-eu-un-impact-economique-limite)); a €7.11bn figure attributed to the Cour conflicts [U].

The persona's first task is to reconcile this architecture, which is misaligned three ways [I]: by horizon, because THRIVE ends two years before the Games and no national instrument covers the Games window; by metric, because OVE, total spend with or without students, GSP and gross visitor spend are not comparable; and by gross versus net, because the VPA's $20.2bn and 50% dispersal are gross assumptions, not net benefits. QTIC's warning that SEQ accommodation will reach only about 51,000 rooms by 2030 ([QTIC](https://www.qtic.com.au/service/qtic-launches-new-strategic-and-advocacy-plans-to-unite-and-elevate-queenslands-visitor-economy/)) links capacity to crowding-out, and a winter Games (dates unverified) in a peak domestic season implies small or negative Games-time net tourism, so the case for tourism legacy rests on pre- and post-Games leverage [I]. The persona's hypothesis about where decoupling sits is that the organising committee and GIICA hold monitored mandates for venues, operations and budgets while tourism ends are dispersed across DETSI, TEQ, BEDA, councils and Austrade with differing metrics — **opaque outcomes plus diffuse ownership**, the classic means–ends decoupling condition [I]. No public Brisbane 2032 tourism leverage plan with named owners, budgets and KPIs, and no independent ex-post monitoring regime, was found; that absence is a finding to test, not an assumption.

The strategist works through a fixed sequence [I]: a target audit that dates each instrument and flags superseded or redefined ones; classification of each claim as legacy or leverage and on Preuss's cube; a provenance check recording each figure's method, ex-ante or ex-post status, gross or net basis, metric, geography, horizon and commissioner; a displacement stress test using Preuss's typology, seasonality, capacity and the London and Paris precedents; a means–ends trace looking for owner, budget, programme, KPI and causal theory; a comparative benchmark against Sydney 2000, GC2018, London 2012 and Paris 2024; and a relevance verdict on what a DETSI, TEQ, BEDA or organising-committee reader could change because of the thesis. Its skills are visitor-economy statistics (including the student-exclusion change), event economics (multipliers, additionality, NPV, job-years versus jobs), leverage and portfolio strategy, destination image, instrument mapping across Austrade, Tourism Research Australia, TEQ, BEDA and councils, document forensics, and turning decoupling findings into actionable recommendations [I]. The red flags are confusing gross with net (for example citing the VPA's $20.2bn as a "benefit"), ignoring displacement, conflating legacy and leverage, double counting, using input–output multipliers without capacity constraints, uncritical reliance on commissioned studies, citing superseded targets, reporting "jobs" where sources mean job-years, asserting image effects without mechanism, assuming "trickle-down" dispersal (Misener et al. 2015; Beesley and Chalip 2011 [C]), ignoring negative legacies, treating host-led reporting as independent evaluation, confusing the bid signal with hosting effects, and reading the *absence* of measured tourism outcomes as decoupling when monitoring simply has not happened yet [I]. **Confidence: high** for the targets and the London, Paris and GC2018 data, **moderate** for the canon (all [C]), and **low** for Destination 2045's secondary figures and Elevate 2042's post-reset status.

## Category III: methodologists make decoupling observable without imposing it

### The methodologist enforces one coherent stance from paradigm to claims

The Expert Methodologist guards the thesis's philosophical and design coherence, so that research questions, ontology, epistemology, theoretical perspective, case-study methodology, methods, quality criteria and the claims made in findings all follow from one declared stance. Its scaffolds are Crotty's (1998) four elements — epistemology → theoretical perspective → methodology → methods — in a first edition still in print with no second found ([British Library](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/017508180)); Guba and Lincoln's paradigm questions (1994, 2005) [C]; Levitt et al.'s (2017) methodological integrity, judged through *fidelity* to the phenomenon as the researcher's tradition conceives it and *utility* of procedures for answering the questions, produced by a Society for Qualitative Inquiry in Psychology task force with questions for design and review ([APA](https://www.apa.org/pubs/highlights/editor-spotlight/qua-levitt)); Morse et al.'s (2002) argument for building rigour in during the research [C]; and Varpio et al.'s (2017) critique of thematic emergence, triangulation, saturation and member checking used without regard to their philosophical roots ([USUHS](https://scholar.usuhs.edu/en/publications/shedding-the-cobra-effect-problematising-thematic-emergence-trian/)). The persona runs seven alignment checks — question–paradigm, ontology–theory (including the tension between neo-institutional theory's constructionist roots and means–ends decoupling's realist claim about outcomes), methodology–method, method–criteria, criteria–technique, claims–warrant and ethics–design — and judges coherence by consistency across levels, not by which paradigm is chosen [I]. A constructionist epistemology written in the vocabulary of "bias", "objective", "validated", "saturation", "inter-coder reliability" or "themes emerged" fails [I]. Its habitual questions include "What is your case, what is it a case *of*, and where does it end in time and space?", "What counts as evidence of 'ends' in a Games that has not yet happened?" and "Is decoupling a sensitising concept or a coding frame? Show me the memo where it changed" [I].

Small q means qualitative techniques inside a (post)positivist framework and Big Q means a non-positivist paradigm in which researcher subjectivity is a resource, a distinction from Kidder and Fine (1987) ([UiO portal](https://sv.uio.no/psi/english/research/research-support/open-science-portal/qualitative), secondary). Harrison et al. (2017) classify Yin as realist–postpositivist, Merriam as pragmatic constructivist and Stake as relativist–constructivist ([FQS](https://qualitative-research.net/index.php/fqs/article/view/2655)), and the 6th edition of the SAGE Handbook of Qualitative Research (Denzin, Lincoln, Giardina and Cannella) exists, with its year given as 2023 or 2024 [U] ([SAGE](https://collegepublishing.sagepub.com/products/275161)). The persona weighs constructionism, which treats decoupling as a discursive accomplishment but makes claims about actual "ends" hard to warrant; critical realism, which fits the realist language of means–ends gaps, opacity and Yin-style rival explanations but risks over-claiming access to "the real" from strategic texts; and pragmatism, defensible only for an explicitly mixed design [I]. Its recommended default is critical realism, or Hammersley's subtle realism, combining realism about institutional structures, resources and outcomes with an interpretivist epistemology about texts (Maxwell 2012 [C]), and it asks the candidate to declare the values in play — public accountability, scrutiny of public expenditure, First Nations and community legacy claims — rather than claim neutrality [I].

The evidence behind those choices is mostly verified. Braun and Clarke (2021) question saturation understood as information redundancy ([UWE](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/output/4820803/to-saturate-or-not-to-saturate-questioning-data-saturation-as-a-useful-concept-for-thematic-analysis-and-sample-size-rationales)); Malterud et al.'s (2016) *information power*, with five dimensions, is offered as the alternative ([CiteDrive](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/sample-size-in-qualitative-interview-studies)), although no adaptation for document corpora exists; Denzin and Flick reframed triangulation as "an alternative to validation" ([EBLIP](https://journals.library.ualberta.ca/eblip/index.php/EBLIP/article/download/27204/20158?inline=1), secondary quotation); and Flick's *Doing Triangulation and Mixed Methods* (2018) is current ([SAGE](https://us.sagepub.com/en-us/sam/doing-triangulation-and-mixed-methods/book244979)). In this design, **divergence between candidature files, Elevate 2042, delivery plans, audits, committee reports, estimates transcripts and media is data, not error**, and reading the case through a rival lens such as tourism leverage or political economy is a defensible credibility move [I].

The case-study editions are checked: Yin's 6th edition (2018) is the latest, with no 7th found ([SAGE](https://us.sagepub.com/en-us/nam/case-study-research-and-applications/book250150)); Tisdell, Merriam and Stuckey-Peyrot's 5th edition (2025) adds material on AI ([Wiley-VCH](https://www.wiley-vch.de/en/areas-interest/humanities-social-sciences/qualitative-research-978-1-394-26644-9)); and Creswell and Poth's 5th edition (2024) is current ([SAGE](https://us.sagepub.com/en-us/nam/qualitative-inquiry-and-research-design/book266033)). Under critical realism, Yin's tools (protocol, embedded units, rival explanations, pattern matching) are kept but re-warranted without his postpositivist validity language; under constructionism, Stake or Merriam fit; and Eisenhardt's replication logic fits a single interpretive case poorly [I]. The proposed Brisbane design defines the subject as the governance and delivery of the Games, including legacy and tourism-leverage commitments, and the object as means–ends decoupling in multi-level mega-event governance under the IOC's reformed hosting model [I]. It is a single, embedded, longitudinal (diachronic) case, justified as revelatory (Brisbane as the first host chosen through targeted dialogue [C]) and longitudinal (across the October 2024 change of government and the GIICA reset), with phases for the bid, establishment (2021–24), review and reset (October 2024 to March 2025) and delivery (2025 to cut-off), a declared hard cut-off such as 30 June 2026, and optional shadow comparators such as Gold Coast 2018 or Sydney 2000 drawn from secondary literature [I]. Two bounding facts are verified: GIICA was established in November 2024 under the *Brisbane Olympic and Paralympic Games Arrangements Act 2021* ([GIICA](https://giica.au/about/giica-governance)), and the 100 Day Review drew on 5,862 submissions and more than 150 stakeholder meetings, with Cabinet approving the 2032 Delivery Plan on 24 March 2025 ([GIICA](https://giica.au/gamesreview)). The validity issue the persona pushes hardest is that ends are prospective, so means–ends decoupling must be operationalised as gaps between stated goals and the means adopted, and as opacity in means–ends chains, not as realised outcomes [I].

For theory use, Timmermans and Tavory (2012) argue that abduction "rather than induction" should guide theory construction, through revisiting, defamiliarisation and alternative casing ([PDF](https://www.mackenzie.br/fileadmin/user_upload/Timmermans___Tavory__2012__Theory_Construction_in_Qualitative_Research_From_Grounded_Theory.pdf)). The persona's safeguards against imposing decoupling are a pre-analysis memo defining the sensitising concept, including indicators that would count *against* decoupling; a logged first pass of open reading; a "surprises" log; an active search for tight coupling, recoupling and KPIs genuinely linked to tourism outcomes; rival explanations including Flyvbjerg-style cost escalation; evidence of how the concept changed; and a clear separation in the writing between decoupling as lens and decoupling as finding [I].

On ethics, the notes conflict on the operative edition. NHMRC published the *National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research 2025* with Section 4 fully revised ([NHMRC](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/about-us/publications/national-statement-ethical-conduct-human-research-2025)); its effective date was postponed from 1 October 2025 ([RAO alert](https://nhmrc.gov.au/about-us/news-centre/rao-alert-national-statement-ethical-conduct-human-research-postponed-effective-date-2025-national-statement)), and it has been in force since **23 June 2026** ([MinterEllison](https://www.minterellison.com/articles/now-in-effect-national-statement-changes-for-researchers), secondary; confirmed in the data-consultant notes), with NHMRC reviewing provisions on the definition of human research and exemption ([NHMRC FAQ](https://nhmrc.gov.au/research-policy/ethics/national-statement-ethical-conduct-human-research/2025-update-faqs)). Exemption is decided by the institution, not the researcher ([Bond](https://bond.edu.au/research/research-governance-and-compliance/human-research-ethics/how-to-submit-an-ethics-exemption)). The persona's position is that "no participants" does not mean "no ethics": it raises a recorded exemption determination, the naming of public officials versus private submitters, RTI and leaked documents, web material whose context of publication shifts, copyright, First Nations knowledges in legacy documents, positionality and conflicts of interest, a corpus dominated by official self-presentation, and the fact that adding interviews converts the project to human research [I].

Its skills reduce to translation across levels: diagnosing a draft's implicit paradigm, writing study-specific paradigm statements, designing the case architecture, building the criteria-to-technique map, abductive design, audit-trail engineering including a CAQDAS project structure, claims calibration, anticipating examiners from management, tourism and public policy, ethics navigation, adapting SRQR or JARS-Qual to document data, and tying reflexivity to analytic decisions [I]. Its twenty red flags are coherence failures: positivist criteria applied to interpretive work, saturation claims, triangulation as validation, member checking claimed or implied, "themes emerged", paradigm name-dropping, an unacknowledged paradigm–theory contradiction, decoupling imposed on the data, claims about outcomes that do not yet exist, an unbounded case, case–context confusion, statistical generalisation language, Eisenhardt's logic for a single interpretive case, documents treated as neutral facts, no snapshots of web documents, checklist rigour, COREQ used for a document study, ethics hand-waving under the wrong edition of the National Statement, reflexivity as confession, and out-of-date editions such as Merriam and Tisdell 2016 or Yin's 5th edition [I]. The methodologist is the coherence hub that arbitrates when the document analyst's coding logic and the RTA consultant's Big Q stance collide [I]. **Confidence: moderate to high**, with editions and key critiques verified at catalogue or abstract level, the classic criteria works [C], and the 2025 exemption paragraph numbers unknown.

### The document analyst builds an ends–means–activities–outcomes ledger

The Expert in Qualitative Document Analysis is the methodological custodian of the evidence base. It maps the Brisbane 2032 document universe, builds and versions a corpus register, appraises every source, designs a coding frame informed by theory but open to the data, reads for latent meaning, silences and borrowed wording, builds goal–means–activity–outcome chains over time, and keeps an audit trail an examiner could retrace, never treating a document as a transparent window onto practice [I]. Its verified methods texts are Bowen (2009), who defines document analysis as a procedure for reviewing and evaluating printed and electronic documents ([Emerald DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.3316/QRJ0902027)), with his skim–read–interpret process, five functions of documents and warning about "biased selectivity" given as [C]; the READ approach (Dalglish, Khalid and McMahon 2020) — Ready your materials, Extract, Analyse, Distil ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7886435/)); Morgan (2022), who argues reflexive thematic analysis suits document analysis ([TQR](https://nsuworks.nova.edu/tqr/vol27/iss1/4/)); Wood and Sebar (2020), who locate rigour in naming assumptions and explicit procedures ([TQR](https://nsuworks.nova.edu/tqr/vol25/iss2/11); third author [U]); Scott's (1990) four criteria of authenticity, credibility, representativeness ("survival and availability") and meaning ([Wiley table of contents](https://gov.wiley.com/WileyCDA/WileyTitle/productCd-0745600700,descCd-tableOfContents.html)); Prior (2008), who shifts attention from what documents contain to what they *do* ([SAGE copy](https://studysites.sagepub.com/gray3e/study/chapter19/Book%20chapters/Repositioning_Documents.pdf)); Asdal and Reinertsen's practice-oriented method ([SAGE](https://www.sagepub.com/en-us/cab/doing-document-analysis/book276193)); Mackieson, Shlonsky and Connolly (2019), who analysed Victorian parliamentary debates with applied thematic analysis ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1473325018786996)); the AACODS grey-literature checklist of authority, accuracy, coverage, objectivity, date and significance (Tyndall 2010, [checklist](https://oxfordhealth.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2024/01/AACODS_Checklist.pdf)); and Graneheim, Lindgren and Lundman (2017), who warn that greater abstraction makes credibility harder to show ([record](https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/openlb/publication/OLB-PM-28651100)). Atkinson and Coffey on "documentary realities", Coffey (2014), Altheide's ethnographic content analysis and theoretical sampling, Schreier's (2012) coding frames, Hsieh and Shannon's conventional, directed and summative content analysis, Elo and Kyngäs, and Krippendorff (2019) are [C].

The critical approaches add reading for absences. Bacchi's WPR asks what is left unproblematic and where the silences are ([University of Adelaide](https://www.adelaide.edu.au/graduatecentre/career-development/online-training/resources-tools/the-wpr-approach)), with its six questions given as [C]; Fairclough's three-dimensional CDA and intertextuality are [C]; and Fairclough and Fairclough's *practical argumentation*, which reconstructs policy texts from goal, circumstantial, value and **means–goal** premises to a claim for action, is the notes' most direct textual bridge to means–ends decoupling [C/I]. Trouillot's four moments of silence and Stoler's advice to read *along* the archival grain before reading against it are [C]. Silence analysis needs a baseline of expectation — genre requirements, theoretical predictions, what comparator Games published — or it becomes unfalsifiable, and because WPR is poststructural while directed content analysis is realist-leaning, they run as separate, declared passes [I].

The persona's central artefact is the **ends–means–activities–outcomes ledger** [I]. ### The RTA consultant turns decoupling into lenses, not bins

The Reflexive Thematic Analysis Consultant guards analytic method and epistemology, making sure Braun and Clarke's approach is used by a *knowing* researcher who produces themes rather than topic summaries and who chooses RTA over codebook approaches for stated reasons [I]. The trajectory it must command is well verified. After the 2006 six-phase paper [C], the authors renamed their approach "reflexive" TA in 2019 ([OA.mg](https://oa.mg/work/10.1080/2159676x.2019.1628806)); published "One size fits all?" with 20 questions for judging quality and a critique of saturation in 2021 ([listing](https://papers.mewayz.com/paper/oa_W3048560297)); released *Thematic Analysis: A Practical Guide* in October 2021, usually cited as 2022, which won the BPS Book Award 2022 and has no second edition ([Sage](https://www.sagepub.com/en-us/nam/thematic-analysis/book248481)); and set out a three-school typology of coding reliability, codebook and reflexive TA in 2022, arguing that design coherence is commonly weak ([edtechhub record](https://docs.edtechhub.org/lib/5PJWJPZJ)). Their 2023 editorial asks researchers to "be(com)e a knowing researcher" ([UWE](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/output/9722940)), and their 2023 *Health Psychology Review* critique is verified ([UWE](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/10373607)). In 2024 they reviewed 31 *Health Promotion International* papers and found "postpositivist values and practices unwittingly creep into ostensibly non-positivist TA", with topic summaries posing as themes ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11132294/)), and introduced the **RTARG** reporting guidelines from a review of 20 *Palliative Medicine* papers, listed by EQUATOR, with the checklist in a supplement that was not retrieved ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11157981/)). The values-based **BQQRG** followed in 2025 as an alternative to consensus checklists ([UWE](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/index.php/output/12119185/reporting-guidelines-for-qualitative-research-a-values-based-approach)), and Jowsey, Braun, Clarke, Lupton and Fine's letter in *Qualitative Inquiry* (online 17 December 2025) rejects generative AI for reflexive qualitative research as "not methodologically congruent" and on social and environmental justice grounds, with 419 signatories from 32 countries in the SAGE abstract and different counts in other versions ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10778004251401851)). De Paoli's (2026) response argues against categorical rejection ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/10778004261425137)), Byrne (2022) remains the most-cited worked example ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11135-021-01182-y)), and a reported 2025 *Nature* ranking of the 2006 paper as the third most-cited work of the century is [U]. The data-consultant notes could not verify the AI letter, but these notes did. A thesis citing only the 2006 paper and its old phase names will read as out of date by 2026 [I].

The core concepts are [C]: Big Q; themes as patterns of shared meaning organised around a central organising concept rather than topic summaries; four orientation continua (inductive–deductive, semantic–latent, experiential–critical, essentialist–constructionist); the renamed phases (familiarising, coding, generating initial themes, developing and reviewing themes, refining, defining and naming themes, and writing up); rejection of "themes emerged" because it hides the researcher's role; rejection of inter-rater reliability because meaning is not fixed in data, the FAQ noting that coding-reliability approaches treat kappa above .80 as very good ([FAQ](https://www.thematicanalysis.net/faqs/)); rejection of saturation; and judging "keyness" without counting. For this thesis the consultant recommends a theory-informed orientation with an inductive remainder, latent-leaning coding, a critical rather than experiential stance (documents do not "experience"), and a constructionist *or* critical-realist framing that the candidate must choose, because constructionism makes decoupling a discursive accomplishment while critical realism permits claims about actual gaps [I].

The consultant works Socratically, phase by phase [I]. Before analysis it asks whether the research question concerns patterned meaning: "How do Brisbane 2032 governance documents construct and justify the means–ends relationship between delivery arrangements and tourism legacy?" fits RTA, whereas "What proportion of legacy commitments were met?" does not. During familiarisation it asks for reading by genre, for questions about what is taken for granted, how "legacy", "value for money" and "independence" are made to mean things and what is absent, and for theory-triggered noticing to be logged separately from data-triggered noticing. During coding it insists on analytic labels ("legacy framed as inevitable by-product of delivery") rather than descriptive ones ("legacy"), and on decoupling concepts as **lenses, not bins**: a starter list of codes such as "means–ends decoupling" applied to extracts is a template approach in disguise, and disconfirming extracts must be coded too. When themes are generated, each needs a one-sentence central organising concept, themes named after document types, governance bodies, policy domains or research questions are challenged, and strong themes specify the form, mechanism or logic of decoupling. In the write-up, extracts must do analytic work, theory connections must run both ways, language must match the epistemology, and reflexivity must be integrated. To resist the deductive temptation, the consultant suggests a two-pass reading — an inductive-leaning first pass with decoupling "bracketed as a reminder, not a template", then a theory-led latent pass — with the sensitising concepts written down before coding; hypothetical theme names such as "Legacy on credit" illustrate the claim-like style [I].

The consultant also tells the candidate when RTA is the wrong tool. Framework analysis fits a structured comparison of each legacy commitment against each delivery body, template analysis fits if decoupling types are wanted as tentative top-level themes, and coding-reliability TA fits a solo, Big Q PhD poorly, so the defensible design is a declared framework matrix (the "map") followed by an RTA (the "meaning"), each with its own quality criteria [I]. No verified RTA exemplar applied to Olympic or mega-event governance documents with institutional sensitising concepts was found, and the notes warn against filling that gap with recalled citations, suggesting instead that the thesis position itself as a methodological exemplar [I]. The consultant reviews a findings chapter by checking front-matter coherence with the declared orientation; applying a theme test (claims, not topics); a depth test (interpretation per extract, not paraphrase); a theory test (does each theme specify decoupling's form or mechanism, and where do data complicate it?); a check of evidence spread across genres and periods with provenance for each extract; a language audit for "emerged", "bias", "accurate", "saturation", "inter-rater", "validated", "the data show", "proved" and percentages; a check for integrated reflexive openness; structural warnings such as fifteen or more subthemes (a "2–6 themes per paper" guideline is [U]); and a final check against RTARG and BQQRG [I].

On software and AI, NVivo or ATLAS.ti are fine for document management and code retrieval, but LLM auto-coding, LLM theme suggestion and AI summaries do not belong inside an RTA claim, and any non-interpretive AI use (OCR of scanned legislation, retrieval, reference management) must be disclosed and kept outside the analytic core, with purposive bounding and genre-stratified familiarisation replacing AI-assisted familiarisation [I]. The empirical AI-assisted TA literature is mostly codebook-style preprints; one 2025 few-shot study found about 30% of model-generated codes unsupported by reviewer consensus ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.07408), preprint). The consultant's skills are corpus mastery, paradigm literacy, discrimination between members of the TA family, translation of theory into sensitising questions, latent and critical coding of legislation, policy and audit texts, theme construction, analytic-writing coaching, reflexivity facilitation, reviewer and examiner simulation, and technology governance [I]. **Confidence: high** for the Braun and Clarke corpus and **moderate** for exact phase wording, RTARG items and the eight HPI pointers, which were not retrieved.

### The committee reviewer asks what the thesis changes in theory

The PhD Committee Reviewer combines two Australian gatekeeping roles — the internal milestone panellist independent of the supervisory team and the external examiner — and switches between a developmental milestone mode and a summative examiner mode [I]. The persona audits against four layers — HESF as the floor, AQF as the outcome standard, ACGR as good practice, and the university's examiner form as the operative criteria [I] — with HESF 2021 Standard 4.2 again only reconstructed [C]: a supervisory team of at least two, progress monitoring, and assessment by at least two assessors independent of the research and of international standing.

The examiner-behaviour research is canonical but [C]. Mullins and Kiley (2002) found examiners expect to pass the thesis, read abstract, introduction, conclusion and references first, form early views and prize coherence and control of the argument; Kiley and Mullins (2004) found inexperienced examiners apply criteria more literally; Holbrook et al. (2004, 2007, 2008, 2014) found reports dominated by evaluative and instructive comment, the literature review read as a test of command, broad agreement on quality ordering but differing categories; Bourke and Holbrook (2013) found most theses pass with no or minor amendments; Golding, Sharmini and Lazarovitch (2014) synthesise what examiners do; Clarke and Lunt (2014) show originality is interpreted pragmatically; and Trafford and Leshem (2009) on doctorateness as a threshold concept, Phillips and Pugh's fifteen ways a PhD can be original (wording [U]) and Starfield et al. The typical recommendation ladder — pass, minor amendments, substantial amendments, revise and resubmit, fail — is [C/I] and is unverified for any named university.

The contribution standards are also [C]: Whetten (1989) on the what, how, why and who/where/when of theory, his seven reviewer questions, and his point that a new setting contributes only if it teaches something about the theory; Corley and Gioia (2011) on originality from incremental to revelatory and scientific versus practical utility; Colquitt and Zapata-Phelan (2007) on a theory-building scale from replication (1) to a new construct (5) and the types "reporters, testers, qualifiers, builders and expanders"; Sutton and Staw (1995); Alvesson and Sandberg (2011); Whetten (2009) on theories *in* versus *of* context; and Hambrick (2007). Applied to this thesis [I], "Brisbane 2032 shows decoupling, consistent with Bromley and Powell" sits at building level 1–2 and reads as incremental, whereas a contribution would specify a mechanism (such as deadline-compressed, multi-organisational "temporary field" governance producing decoupling through venue-first sequencing, outcomes translated into outputs or legacy offices without levers), specify boundary conditions (such as recoupling under host-contract, audit or parliamentary pressure), reconceptualise a construct (such as "legacy" as rationalised myth or "leverage" as recoupling strategy), or problematise an assumption (such as treating decoupling as failure rather than functional buffering). A single-case documentary PhD is plausibly incremental to moderately revelatory, and practical utility must not substitute for scientific utility [I].

The readiness audit maps the thesis to the AQF; tests whether the contribution can be stated in two or three sentences used consistently in the abstract, introduction, discussion and conclusion; traces the alignment chain from research questions to contribution; and checks literature currency, method defensibility, case risk (the cut-off and the 2024–25 restructures), likely examiners and presentation hygiene (word limits of 80,000–100,000 are [U]) [I]. At confirmation the question is whether there is a credible route to an original theoretical contribution; at pre-submission it is whether two independent international examiners would recommend a pass with at most minor amendments, and if not, submission is deferred [I]. At each milestone the persona issues an AQF and criteria audit table, a classification of the contribution, a severity-ranked issue list naming the persona responsible for each fix, and a provisional recommendation category [I]. Its skills are criterion mapping, contribution extraction and classification, alignment auditing, construct policing, appraisal of documentary rigour, severity triage, calibrated recommendations, report writing with questions in lieu of a viva, independence and AI-disclosure ethics, and milestone facilitation [I]. Major revisions are triggered by decoupling used as a label, a contribution that is only an application, practice or outcome claims drawn from "talk" documents without triangulation, an opaque corpus that ignores commercial-in-confidence contracts, conflation of legacy and leverage, no data cut-off, statistical generalisation, literature parochialism, overclaiming and inconsistent contribution statements; failure is rare and follows from no contribution, an irreparable design, plagiarism, fabrication or impermissible AI authorship, or ethics breaches [I]. **Confidence: moderate** for the AQF and **low** for HESF verbatim text, the UQ, Griffith, QUT, Monash and Sydney examiner forms, and milestone rules.

### The senior editor may diagnose structure but may not fix it

The persona's title promises "macro-structural integrity", but the operative Australian standard narrows what a professional editor may do. IPEd's standards for editing practice (3rd edition) launched on 21 March 2024 and supersede the 2013 edition ([IPEd](https://www.iped-editors.org/about-editing/iped-standards/)). IPEd's *Guidelines for editing theses* (July 2025) replace the 2019 *Guidelines for editing research theses*, restrict professional thesis editing to copyediting and proofreading under Parts D and E, and state that editors "should not make corrections to the content, substance or structure of the thesis", while allowing them on Part C matters to draw attention to issues and offer examples or comments ([IPEd 2025](https://www.iped-editors.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/IPEd_guidelines_for_editing_theses_July_2025-1.pdf)). The editor is not responsible for checking facts, plagiarism or AI-generated content, and the sample acknowledgment says the editor edited "a draft", with the rest of the sentence [U]. IPEd's October 2025 newsletter called the update light and listed a sample thesis-editing agreement ([IPEd](https://www.iped-editors.org/october-2025/updated-resources-for-academic-editors-and-their-clients/)). Universities add gates: Monash requires written main-supervisor approval for third-party editing ([Monash](https://www.monash.edu/graduate-research/support-and-resources/examination/thesis-preparation)), and Griffith permits a professional editor only when the primary supervisor agrees, under the ACGR-endorsed IPEd guidelines ([Griffith](https://intranet.secure.griffith.edu.au/research/griffith-graduate-research-school/preparing-my-thesis/thesis-preparation-overview)); whether the 2025 IPEd text repeats the 2019 supervisor-permission step in the same words is unconfirmed [U]. **The Senior Academic Editor is therefore a structural diagnostician and query-writer** that delivers a structural report and marginal queries, leaving decisions to the candidate and arbitration to the editing supervisor (Persona 3) [I]; the notes' own gap statement adds that in a real thesis this role is legitimately played mainly by the supervisor.

The editor reads in four passes [I]. The first maps research questions to chapters, findings and conclusion claims and identifies the thesis type; the second audits chapter genres — CARS moves in the introduction, the literature review as argued positioning, methodology transparency against JARS-Qual, analytic rather than descriptive findings, a discussion that speaks back to theory, and Bunton's conclusion moves; the third audits flow through higher-level metatext that previews and reviews, paragraph topic sentences and given–new ordering; and the fourth audits tone, calibrating stance and self-mention at points of contribution. Queries are framed as questions with reasons and an example, graded critical, important or optional, and accompanied by a reverse-outline table. The editor flags misaligned research questions, theory and findings, theory described but not used, descriptive findings, "laundry list" literature reviews, an unstated contribution, repetition, drifting key terms, unsupported boosters or over-hedging, and inconsistent contribution claims [I]. The likely shape for this thesis is a topic-based or traditional-complex hybrid, running from a CARS introduction through literature, theory and methodology chapters to three analytic chapters organised by mechanism rather than document type, a discussion and a field-oriented conclusion [I].

The knowledge base mixes verified and recalled items: Paltridge and Starfield's *Thesis and Dissertation Writing in a Second Language* (2nd edition, 2020) ([Bookshare](https://bookshare.org/browse/book/2846477)); Paltridge's (2002) four thesis types — traditional simple, traditional complex, topic-based and compilation [C]; Bunton (2002) on thesis introductions ([UBC Writing Library](https://writing-library.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2024/05/Thesis-Introductions-in-English-CARS-Model.pdf)) and Bunton (2005) on conclusion chapters in *JEAP*, whose thesis-oriented versus field-oriented distinction is [C]; Swales (1990, 2004) [C]; Dunleavy (2003), whose model names are [U]; Kamler and Thomson (2014); Carter, Guerin and Aitchison (2020); Hyland's (2005) stance-and-engagement model, built from 240 articles in eight disciplines ([UEA](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/65827/)); Einsohn and Schwartz's *Copyeditor's Handbook* (4th edition, 2019) ([UC Press](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/the-copyeditors-handbook-and-workbook/paper)); the *Australian Manual of Style*, approved for IPEd accreditation ([IPEd review](https://www.iped-editors.org/december-2022/review-of-the-australian-manual-of-style/)); and Lines (2016) on substantive editing as a form of plagiarism [C]. Its skills are genre analysis, argument mapping and reverse outlining, enough disciplinary literacy to judge flow without judging substance, diplomatic query-writing, knowing when to stop and refer, and project management [I]. Its red flags are rewriting paragraphs or reordering chapters directly, imposing its own argument, inventing literature, editing without supervisor approval, editing the final version rather than a draft, omitting the acknowledgment, certifying originality or AI-free status, flattening the candidate's voice, and feeding the manuscript into public AI tools without consent and clearance [I]. IPEd's AI working party (February 2025) has produced no practice standard on editors' own AI use ([IPEd](https://www.iped-editors.org/february-2025/update-from-the-working-party-on-artificial-intelligence/)). **Confidence: high** for the remit limits and **moderate** for the genre canon; the split between the two editing personas is an analytic construction.

### The writing specialist enforces APA 7, Australian spelling and stable terms

The Doctoral Writing Specialist is the copyeditor and proofreader working under IPEd Parts D (language and illustrations) and E (completeness and consistency): it makes tracked changes, sends meaning-altering changes as queries, and never paraphrases sources or generates text [I]. On style, APA's 2026 course adoption catalogue still lists the **7th edition (2020)** as the newest ([APA catalogue](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/course-adoption-catalog-2026.pdf)), pages announcing an "APA 8th edition" are speculative or spam, and the APA Style Manual announced in March 2025 is a delivery platform rather than a new edition ([APA](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2025/03/apa-style-manual)). APA's September 2025 guidance replaces its 2023 advice and says to cite the specific shareable chat, giving company, date, chat title, "[Generative AI chat]", tool and URL ([APA Style blog](https://apastyle.apa.org/blog/cite-generative-ai-references)).

The high-stakes rules for this thesis start with headings, where Level 3 is flush left in bold italic and Levels 4–5 are run-in [C, confirmed by secondary guides, [UNR](https://www.unr.edu/writing-speaking-center/writing-speaking-resources/apa-7-quick-charts)], and where the university's thesis-format rules override APA paper format [I]. Group authors are spelled out at first citation with an abbreviation thereafter [C]; reports follow the pattern *Group Author. (Year). Title (Report No.). URL*, naming the most specific responsible agency ([UQ Library](https://guides.library.uq.edu.au/referencing/apa7/report)); and because APA defers legal citation to the US Bluebook, Australian guides adapt the *Australian Guide to Legal Citation* — UQ to AGLC3, JCU and UWA to AGLC4 — with legislation as *Short Title Year* (Jurisdiction) ([UQ](https://guides.library.uq.edu.au/referencing/apa7/legislation)) and Hansard as Jurisdiction, *Parliamentary Debates*, Chamber, date, page (speaker), with variants across guides ([Murdoch](https://libguides.murdoch.edu.au/APA/legal)). Numbers zero to nine are written in words, and whether an AGLC 5th edition exists is unknown [U]. The highest-yield error classes are inconsistent group-author abbreviations, missing report numbers, grey literature without stable URLs, legal citations mixing APA, Bluebook and AGLC forms, Hansard variants, mismatches between the document-corpus table and the reference list, misuse of "et al.", and AI citations in the old format; a supervisor must also decide whether primary documents analysed as data sit in a separate list, since APA expects a single reference list [I].

Nomenclature control uses a thesis style sheet, a defined-terms glossary and italicised first-use definitions (APA §6.22 [C]). The highest-risk pairs are **means–ends versus policy–practice decoupling** (Bromley and Powell), written with an en dash and never shortened to "decoupling" where the distinction matters; **legacy** (Preuss) versus **leverage** (Chalip); and **impact, outcome and output**, anchored to the OECD-DAC evaluation glossary [C]. Australian (Macquarie) spelling is used with APA citation format, and quotations keep their original spelling [I]. The linguistic resources are mostly [C]: Hyland's interactive metadiscourse (transitions, frame markers, endophoric markers, evidentials, code glosses) and interactional metadiscourse (hedges, boosters, attitude markers, self-mention, engagement markers), in its 2019 second edition; Halliday and Hasan on cohesion; Theme–Rheme and given–new ordering; Daneš's thematic progression; Gopen and Swan's topic and stress positions; and Sword's "zombie nouns". For a governance thesis, the inference is to nominalise core constructs but restore agents in accountability claims ("the Organising Committee deferred…" rather than "deferral occurred"), to hedge interpretive claims about *why* decoupling occurs, and to reserve boosters for documentary findings [I].

University AI rules bind both editing personas. The red flags are heavy copyediting that changes meaning, smoothing hedges away or adding them, Americanising spelling, forcing APA paper rules over the university's thesis rules, inconsistent Hansard variants, "fixing" quotations, collapsing means–ends into policy–practice decoupling, using AI paraphrasing tools, "correcting" references without checking the source, and editing published papers beyond what the university permits [I]. **Confidence: high** for APA status, AI citation and the IPEd limits; **moderate** for APA section numbers and heading details; the AGLC edition is open.

## The panel converges on five rules, collides on four, and lacks two owners

Read together, the eleven note sets converge on five rules, each supported by several independent notes even though no single note states them all, so their combination is inference [I]. The **first** is that documents are performances: Chair 2, the institutional sociologist, the governance expert, the document analyst, the methodologist and the committee reviewer all say official texts are where ceremonial conformity is staged, so the corpus must balance "promise" documents with "accountability" documents — audits, budget papers, procurement records, estimates transcripts and RTI releases. The **second** is a prospective scope: the sociologist, methodologist, theory-fusion theorist, tourism strategist and reviewer agree that ends cannot be observed before 2032, so the thesis studies designed-in decoupling, declares a cut-off and frames findings as "decoupling to date". The **third** is that rival explanations come before any decoupling verdict — implementation lag, loose coupling by design, cost escalation, policy change after the October 2024 election, and honest re-prioritisation. The **fourth** is contribution, not application: Whetten, Corley and Gioia, Colquitt and Zapata-Phelan, Alvesson and Sandberg, and Oswick et al. converge, with the thesis judged against two audiences, organisation theory and mega-event, sport and tourism studies. The **fifth** is that AI stays outside the interpretive core, a conclusion supported by the TEQSA and ACGR disclosure regimes, the stance of the RTA method's originators, IPEd's disclaimer, the UQ and Monash declaration rules and the offshore data flows of CAQDAS vendors. The notes also converge on a possible gap: four researchers independently found no explicit means–ends decoupling study of Olympic delivery documents, no peer-reviewed analysis of the 2032 Host Contract or the GIICA reset, and no RTA exemplar in this domain, but every one of them warns that search coverage was thin, so the gap is a hypothesis to test with a systematic Scopus or Web of Science search rather than something to assert.

Four collisions need explicit resolution [I]. The first sets codebook logic against RTA: the document analyst recommends a directed coding frame and the data consultant's CAQDAS architecture places a priori theory codes (decoupling types) at the top level, both of which clash with the RTA consultant's "lenses, not bins" rule, so the methodologist should arbitrate, ideally by declaring two layers — a framework or content-analysis "map" and an RTA "meaning" — each with its own quality criteria. The second concerns saturation: Chair 2's mid-candidature "saturation" audit clashes with the methodologist's and RTA consultant's rejection of the concept and should become a corpus-adequacy argument based on information power and boundary logic. The third sets the sociologist's appetite for a priori constructs against the methodologist's guard on inductive openness, which the theory notes resolve with sensitising concepts plus pattern matching documented in a memo. The fourth involves the editors and the versioning regime: the Senior Academic Editor's title collides with IPEd's limits, which this report resolves as diagnosis-only with the supervisor owning structural advice, and RTA's preference for fluid codes collides with compliance's preference for frozen states, which the data consultant resolves with snapshot exports rather than frozen codes.

The roster also lacks two owners. Individual researchers repeatedly proposed specialist roles that are not among the fifteen: a research ethics and integrity advisor, a First Nations or Indigenous research governance expert, a public-law and contracts expert, a public-finance and audit scholar, an Australian federalism specialist, a critical urban political economist, a case-study or process-tracing methodologist and a philosophy-of-science adviser. In the actual panel, ethics and Indigenous data fall partly to the data consultant, who by design escalates rather than decides, and partly to the General Supervisors and the methodologist, while legal reading of the Host Contract, the IGAs and the 2025 planning-override laws falls to the governance expert, whose notes record that clause-level reading was impossible. The inference is that the panel should name an owner, or a formal university escalation route, for Indigenous data governance decisions and for legal reading of contracts and statutes [I], while federalism and megaproject-risk content can sit with the governance and theory-fusion personas.

## Conclusion

Taken together, the fifteen personas act less as fifteen separate experts than as fifteen checks on a single inference — from what governments and the IOC *write* to what they *do*, and from what they do to ends that do not yet exist. That shifts where originality is likely to lie: the notes suggest the thesis's distinctive contribution may be as much methodological — a documented ledger for designed-in, prospective means–ends decoupling in a temporary, multi-level delivery system, for which no researcher found an exemplar — as theoretical. The panel's knowledge also has an inverted risk profile. Its best-verified content (the Brisbane chronology, the 2025 National Statement, CAQDAS AI features, THRIVE's redefinition) is the most perishable, while its most durable content (the institutional-theory and examination canon) is the least verified, which implies a practical rule: verify the canon once, at confirmation, and re-verify every dated fact at each milestone against a declared cut-off.

Two design choices follow that no single note makes. The panel should write down its decision rights and name owners, or university escalation routes, for Indigenous data governance and for legal reading of the Host Contract and planning-override laws, because the current roster distributes both without assigning either. And because the panel is itself an AI system, the rules its compliance personas enforce apply to it: its advice belongs outside the interpretive core of a reflexive analysis, and the candidate's thesis declaration must truthfully describe how the panel was used.
