## Q0. Who is the persona, and how do such theorists behave: how they build, blend and bound theory, the questions they ask, and the artefacts they produce?

### Takeaway
The Senior Academic Theorist is the thesis's theoretical architect. They decide which public-management and sociological theories may be combined, and they check that the assumptions of each are compatible (commensurability). They name the bridging constructs and spell out the causal mechanisms that link governance structures to decoupled outcomes. They also hold the claimed contribution to an AQF Level 10 standard of originality. Their defining habits come from the theorizing-craft literature. They work by abduction and problematization rather than gap-spotting. They choose an explicit form of theorizing (propositions, process model, typology or configuration) instead of a boilerplate "conceptual framework". They state scope and boundary conditions so the fused theory has limits.

**How they build theory (theorizing as process)**
- [VC] Weick treats theory construction as "disciplined imagination", an artificial analogue of evolution. The theorist generates many conjectures through "thought trials" (variation). Selection criteria such as plausibility, interest and connection to existing knowledge then decide which survive and are kept. More heterogeneous conjectures and more diverse criteria tend to yield better theory — Weick, K. E. (1989). Theory construction as disciplined imagination. *Academy of Management Review*, 14(4), 516–531. [doi](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1989.4308376)
- [VC] "Theorizing" is a process, distinct from "theory" as a finished product, and a partial theory still has value — Weick, K. E. (1995). What theory is not, theorizing is. *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 40(3), 385–390.
- [VC] Abductive analysis means building theory from surprising empirical findings set against a broad base of existing theories. It relies on revisiting the data, defamiliarization and "alternative casing", and it is offered as an alternative to inductive grounded theory — Timmermans, S., & Tavory, I. (2012). Theory construction in qualitative research: From grounded theory to abductive analysis. *Sociological Theory*, 30(3), 167–186. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0735275112457914); book-length version: Tavory, I., & Timmermans, S. (2014). *Abductive Analysis: Theorizing Qualitative Research*. University of Chicago Press.
- [VC] Swedberg's sociological guide to theorizing stresses the "context of discovery". It runs from observation and a "prestudy", through naming the phenomenon and forming concepts, to building out the theory with metaphors, analogies, typologies and explanations, often in the form of mechanisms — Swedberg, R. (2014). *The Art of Social Theory*. Princeton University Press. See also Swedberg, R. (Ed.) (2014). *Theorizing in Social Science: The Context of Discovery*. Stanford University Press. [U on chapter-level detail]
- [VC] Problematization challenges the assumptions behind existing theory instead of "gap-spotting". Alvesson and Sandberg type those assumptions as in-house, root-metaphor, paradigmatic, ideological and field assumptions — Alvesson, M., & Sandberg, J. (2011). Generating research questions through problematization. *Academy of Management Review*, 36(2), 247–271. [doi](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.2009.0188)
- [VC] Process theorizing from qualitative process data has seven sense-making strategies: narrative, quantification, alternate templates, grounded theory, visual mapping, temporal bracketing and synthetic. Alternate templates (applying competing theories to the same data) is the strategy closest to theory fusion — Langley, A. (1999). Strategies for theorizing from process data. *Academy of Management Review*, 24(4), 691–710. [doi](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1999.2553248)
- [VC] Klag, M., & Langley, A. (2013). Approaching the conceptual leap in qualitative research. *International Journal of Management Reviews*, 15(2), 149–166. They frame the conceptual leap as a set of dialectical tensions: deliberation versus serendipity, engagement versus detachment, knowing versus not knowing, and self-expression versus social connection.
- [VC] Doubt is a generative resource in theorizing — Locke, K., Golden-Biddle, K., & Feldman, M. S. (2008). Making doubt generative: Rethinking the role of doubt in the research process. *Organization Science*, 19(6), 907–918.

- [VC] Multiparadigm theory building works through "transition zones" between paradigms — Gioia, D. A., & Pitre, E. (1990). Multiparadigm perspectives on theory building. *Academy of Management Review*, 15(4), 584–602. [doi](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1990.4310758)

**How they bound theory**
- [VC] Bacharach defines theory as a system of constructs and variables, bounded by the theorist's assumptions about values, time and space. He sets falsifiability and utility as evaluation criteria — Bacharach, S. B. (1989). Organizational theories: Some criteria for evaluation. *Academy of Management Review*, 14(4), 496–515. [doi](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1989.4308374)
- [VC] Boundary conditions can be discovered and made explicit — Busse, C., Kach, A. P., & Wagner, S. M. (2017). Boundary conditions: What they are, how to explore them, why we need them, and when to consider them. *Organizational Research Methods*, 20(4), 574–609. [U pages]
- [VC] Ostrom separates three levels of specificity: **frameworks** (general sets of variables and a meta-theoretical language), **theories** (which say which variables matter and how) and **models** (precise assumptions about a limited set of parameters). This ladder is useful for keeping a "conceptual framework" from being presented as a theory — Ostrom, E. (2005). *Understanding Institutional Diversity*. Princeton University Press; Ostrom, E. - [VC] Theories of the middle range sit between working hypotheses and grand theory — Merton, R. K. (1968). *Social Theory and Social Structure* (enlarged ed.). Free Press.

**Artefacts they produce**

## Q1. Which public administration and management paradigms would they draw on?

### Takeaway
The theorist treats the PA paradigms (traditional PA/Weberian bureaucracy, NPM, NPG, public value, and post-NPM/whole-of-government) as **layered, coexisting repertoires**, not a historical sequence of replacements. Each brings distinct theoretical roots. Osborne roots NPG explicitly in institutional and network theory, which is the main justification for fusing it with organizational sociology. Each paradigm also brings its own candidate mechanisms: disaggregation, performance contracting, network interdependence, metagovernance, and accountability to forums. For a Games delivery body (an arm's-length, time-limited, special-purpose authority), agencification, depoliticisation and accountability theory are the most directly usable parts.

### Cited Findings
**Traditional public administration / Weberian bureaucracy**
- [VC] The politics–administration dichotomy — Wilson, W. (1887). The study of administration. *Political Science Quarterly*, 2(2), 197–222.
- [VC] Simon's critique of administrative "principles" as contradictory proverbs, which set PA on the road to organizational decision theory — Simon, H. A. (1946). The proverbs of administration. *Public Administration Review*, 6(1), 53–67; Simon, H. A. (1947). *Administrative Behavior*. Macmillan.
- [VC] Bureaucratic organization is defended as an institution with its own values (impartiality, legality, procedural rationality), not just a tool — Olsen, J. P. (2006). Maybe it is time to rediscover bureaucracy. *Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory*, 16(1), 1–24.
- [VC] The "old" institutionalist lineage lies inside PA. Selznick's study of the Tennessee Valley Authority, a special-purpose public delivery authority, theorised co-optation and the displacement of official goals. It is a direct ancestor of means–ends decoupling — Selznick, P. (1949). *TVA and the Grass Roots*. University of California Press. Goal displacement: Merton, R. K. (1940). Bureaucratic structure and personality. *Social Forces*, 18(4), 560–568.

**New Public Management (NPM)**
- [VC] Hood names seven NPM doctrines: hands-on professional management; explicit standards and performance measures; greater emphasis on output controls; disaggregation of units; greater competition; private-sector styles of management; and discipline and parsimony in resource use. He traces NPM to a marriage of new institutional economics (public choice, transaction-cost and principal–agent theory) with business-type managerialism. He also sets out three administrative value families: sigma (lean and purposeful), theta (honest and fair) and lambda (robust and resilient) — Hood, C. (1991). A public management for all seasons? *Public Administration*, 69(1), 3–19. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9299.1991.tb00779.x)
- [VC] NPM produces paradoxes and unintended effects — Hood, C., & Peters, G. (2004). The middle aging of New Public Management: Into the age of paradox? *JPART*, 14(3), 267–282.
- [VC] Administrative doctrines work as persuasive, rhetorical "philosophies", which links PA to the myth and legitimacy arguments of institutional theory — Hood, C., & Jackson, M. (1991). *Administrative Argument*. Dartmouth.
- [VC] Gaming of targets is a concrete mechanism by which performance regimes separate measured means from intended ends. Bevan and Hood identify ratchet effects, threshold effects and output distortion — Bevan, G., & Hood, C. (2006). What's measured is what matters: Targets and gaming in the English public health care system. *Public Administration*, 84(3), 517–538.
- [VC] "Digital-era governance" is offered as NPM's successor, built on reintegration, needs-based holism and digitisation — Dunleavy, P., Margetts, H., Bastow, S., & Tinkler, J. (2006). New Public Management is dead—long live digital-era governance. *JPART*, 16(3), 467–494. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopart/mui057)
- [VC] Hood, C., & Dixon, R. (2015). *A Government that Worked Better and Cost Less? Evaluating Three Decades of Reform and Change in UK Central Government*. Oxford University Press.

**New Public Governance (NPG) and public service logic**
- [VC] Osborne presents NPG as a plural state (many interdependent actors deliver public services) and a pluralist state (many processes shape policy). It focuses on inter-organizational relationships, the governance of processes and outcomes. Osborne contrasts the roots of PA (political science and public policy), NPM (rational/public choice and management studies) and NPG (institutional and network theory) — Osborne, S. P. (2006). The New Public Governance? *Public Management Review*, 8(3), 377–387. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/14719030600853022); Osborne, S. P. (Ed.) (2010). *The New Public Governance? Emerging Perspectives on the Theory and Practice of Public Governance*. Routledge.
- [VC] Later developments in the same line: Osborne, S. P., Radnor, Z., & Nasi, G. (2013). A new theory for public service management? Toward a (public) service-dominant approach. *American Review of Public Administration*, 43(2), 135–158; Osborne, S. P. (2021). *Public Service Logic*. Routledge.

**Public value**
- [VC] Moore's "strategic triangle" says managers create public value by aligning three things: the substantive value produced, the legitimacy and support of the authorizing environment, and operational capacity — Moore, M. H. (1995). *Creating Public Value: Strategic Management in Government*. Harvard University Press; Moore, M. H. (2013). *Recognizing Public Value*. Harvard University Press.
- [VC] "Public value governance" is proposed as the approach after traditional PA and NPM. It emphasises public values, the public sphere, and government as guarantor of public values within multi-sector arrangements — Bryson, J. M., Crosby, B. C., & Bloomberg, L. (2014). Public value governance: Moving beyond traditional public administration and the New Public Management. *Public Administration Review*, 74(4), 445–456. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/puar.12238); Bryson, J. M., Crosby, B. C., & Bloomberg, L. (Eds.) (2015). *Public Value and Public Administration*. Georgetown University Press.
- [VC] Public values (plural, normative consensus) are distinguished from Moore's public value (managerial creation) — Bozeman, B. (2007). *Public Values and Public Interest*. Georgetown University Press.
- [VC] Australian critiques matter for an AQF thesis. Rhodes and Wanna argue that public value can license unelected managers ("platonic guardians") and fits poorly with Westminster responsible government. Other Australian authors review its contested meanings and the claim that it is a paradigm — Rhodes, R. A. W., & Wanna, J. (2007). **Network governance and metagovernance**
- [VC] Rhodes describes governance as self-organizing interorganizational networks. They are marked by interdependence, continuing interaction, game-like exchanges rooted in trust, and significant autonomy from the state, and they contribute to the "hollowing out" of the state — Rhodes, R. - [VC] Bevir and Rhodes later take an interpretive, "decentred" turn — Bevir, M., & Rhodes, R. A. W. (2003). *Interpreting British Governance*. Routledge.
- [VC] A structural counter-argument comes from sport policy. Goodwin and Grix propose "asymmetrical network governance": the state keeps hierarchical control behind network rhetoric, so they bring structures back in — Goodwin, M., & Grix, J. (2011). Bringing structures back in: The governance narrative, the decentred approach and asymmetrical network governance in the education and sport policy communities. *Public Administration*, 89(2), 537–556.
- [VC] Three modes of network governance (participant-governed, lead-organization and network administrative organization), with contingencies of trust, number of participants, goal consensus and the need for network-level competencies — Provan, K. G., & Kenis, P. (2008). Modes of network governance: Structure, management, and effectiveness. *JPART*, 18(2), 229–252. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopart/mum015)
- [VC] Metagovernance is the "governance of governance". Its tools are network design, framing, management and participation, used in hands-off and hands-on forms — Sørensen, E., & Torfing, J. (2009). Making governance networks effective and democratic through metagovernance. *Public Administration*, 87(2), 234–258. - [VC] Klijn, E.-H., & Koppenjan, J. (2016). *Governance Networks in the Public Sector*. Routledge.

**Collaborative governance**
- [VC] Ansell and Gash define collaborative governance as "a governing arrangement where one or more public agencies directly engage non-state stakeholders in a collective decision-making process that is formal, consensus-oriented, and deliberative and that aims to make or implement public policy or manage public programs or assets." They derived a contingency model by "successive approximation" across 137 cases. The model covers starting conditions (power, resource and knowledge asymmetries; incentives; prehistory of conflict or cooperation), institutional design, facilitative leadership, and a cyclical collaborative process (face-to-face dialogue, trust-building, commitment, shared understanding, intermediate outcomes) — Ansell, C., & Gash, A. (2008). Collaborative governance in theory and practice. *JPART*, 18(4), 543–571. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopart/mum032)
- [VC] An "integrative framework" built as nested dimensions: system context, then drivers (leadership, consequential incentives, interdependence, uncertainty), then a collaborative governance regime. The regime's collaboration dynamics are principled engagement, shared motivation and capacity for joint action, leading to actions, impacts and adaptation. The authors define collaborative governance as "the processes and structures of public policy decision making and management that engage people constructively across the boundaries of public agencies, levels of government, and/or the public, private and civic spheres in order to carry out a public purpose that could not otherwise be accomplished" — Emerson, K., Nabatchi, T., & Balogh, S. (2012). An integrative framework for collaborative governance. *JPART*, 22(1), 1–29. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopart/mur011)
- [VC] Ansell, C., & Torfing, J. (2021). *Public Governance as Co-creation*. Cambridge University Press.

**Accountability**
- [VC] Bovens defines accountability as "a relationship between an actor and a forum, in which the actor has an obligation to explain and to justify his or her conduct, the forum can pose questions and pass judgement, and the actor may face consequences." He classifies it by forum (political, legal, administrative, professional, social), by actor (corporate, hierarchical, collective, individual) and by the nature of the obligation (vertical, diagonal, horizontal). He offers democratic, constitutional and learning perspectives for assessing it — Bovens, M. (2007). Analysing and assessing accountability: A conceptual framework. *European Law Journal*, 13(4), 447–468. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0386.2007.00378.x)
- [VC] Related work: Bovens, M., Schillemans, T., & 't Hart, P. (2008). Does public accountability work? An assessment tool. *Public Administration*, 86(1), 225–242; Bovens, M., Goodin, R. E., & Schillemans, T. (Eds.) (2014). *The Oxford Handbook of Public Accountability*. Oxford University Press.
- [VC] "Multiple accountabilities disorder" in hybrid, quasi-governmental bodies. Koppell's five dimensions are transparency, liability, controllability, responsibility and responsiveness. This applies directly to organising committees, which are hybrid public–private — Koppell, J. G. S. (2005). Pathologies of accountability: ICANN and the challenge of "multiple accountabilities disorder". *Public Administration Review*, 65(1), 94–108; Koppell, J. G. S. (2003). *The Politics of Quasi-Government*. Cambridge University Press.
- [VC] Horizontal accountability as a remedy for agency accountability deficits — Schillemans, T. (2011). Does horizontal accountability work? *Administration & Society*, 43(4), 387–416.

## Q2. What does the methodological literature say about the craft of theorizing and theory combination?

### Takeaway
The literature agrees on four points. First, a contribution is judged by what it adds to *theory*: new constructs, relationships, mechanisms or boundary conditions, not new settings (Whetten; Corley & Gioia). Second, combining theories works best when the theorist audits assumptions, defines constructs precisely and lets the combination change both source theories (Okhuysen & Bonardi; Suddaby 2010; Oswick et al.). Third, problematization and abduction are better engines of originality than gap-spotting or straight induction (Alvesson & Sandberg; Timmermans & Tavory). Fourth, theory is plural in form, so the theorist must say which form they are producing (Cornelissen 2017; Cornelissen, Höllerer & Seidl 2021; Abend 2008).

He argues that applying a theory in a new setting is a contribution only if the setting teaches us something new about the theory, for example by changing its boundary conditions — Whetten, D. A.   8. State what the combination changes in each parent theory.

## Q3. How do theorists explain via mechanisms?

### Takeaway
Mechanism-based explanation names the entities, activities and organized sequences that produce outcomes. It is the standard way to turn a fused framework from a list of factors into an explanation. The persona can draw on four families: **analytical sociology** (Hedström & Swedberg; Hedström & Ylikoski; Coleman's macro–micro–macro "boat"), **pragmatist or relational** alternatives (Gross; Davis & Marquis on mechanisms in institutional fields), **context-sensitive political science** (Falleti & Lynch; process tracing per Beach & Pedersen), and **critical realism and historical institutionalism** (Bhaskar, Archer, Sayer; Pierson; Capoccia & Kelemen; Mahoney & Thelen; plus structuration per Giddens and its institutional bridge in Barley & Tolbert). These families make different ontological commitments, and the thesis must choose among them and say so.

### Cited Findings
**Analytical-sociology mechanisms**
- [VC] Hedström and Swedberg offer mechanisms as middle-range explanations and type them as situational (macro to micro), action-formation (micro to micro) and transformational (micro to macro) — Hedström, P., & Swedberg, R. (Eds.) (1998). *Social Mechanisms: An Analytical Approach to Social Theory*. Cambridge University Press; Hedström, P. (2005). *Dissecting the Social*. Cambridge University Press; Coleman, J. S. (1990). *Foundations of Social Theory*. Belknap/Harvard.
- [VC] Mechanisms consist of entities and activities organized to produce a type of outcome regularly. Mechanism-based explanation is an alternative to covering-law explanation, mechanisms are not intervening variables, and the authors defend "structural individualism" — Hedström, P., & Ylikoski, P. (2010). Causal mechanisms in the social sciences. *Annual Review of Sociology*, 36, 49–67. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.soc.012809.102632)
- [VC] Mechanisms in macro-phenomena — Mayntz, R. (2004). Mechanisms in the analysis of social macro-phenomena. *Philosophy of the Social Sciences*, 34(2), 237–259. Political-process mechanisms (environmental, cognitive, relational) — Tilly, C. (2001). Mechanisms in political processes. *Annual Review of Political Science*, 4, 21–41. **Critical realism (structural, generative mechanisms)**
- [VC] Bhaskar's stratified ontology has three domains: the real (structures and mechanisms with causal powers), the actual (events) and the empirical (experiences). **Structuration and its institutional bridge**
- [VC] Giddens's structuration theory holds that structure has a dual character: rules and resources are both medium and outcome of practice. ## Q4. What published work fuses institutional theory with public management or governance, including in sport or mega-event governance, and how did authors justify commensurability?

### Takeaway
There are strong precedents in public administration for fusing institutional theory with public management. Examples are Christensen et al.'s instrument–culture–myth model, Polzer et al.'s logics-based layering of PA paradigms, Skelcher & Smith on hybridity, Ashworth, Boyne & Delbridge and Frumkin & Galaskiewicz on public-sector isomorphism, and Pollitt et al. on "agency fever". Authors usually justify the fusion in one of four ways: **ontological mapping** (paradigms as institutional logics), **shared ancestry** (Simon, March and Selznick), **division of explanatory labour** (each lens explains different variance), or **a meta-theory** (critical realism; structuration). Sport and mega-event research borrows governance theories (network governance, principal–agent, tools of government, regulatory capitalism, wicked problems with critical realism). It rarely gives explicit commensurability arguments, and that weakness is an opening for the PhD.

### Cited Findings
**Public administration fusions**
- [VW] Polzer, Meyer, Höllerer and Seiwald treat the three PA paradigms as institutional logics: bureaucracy (Weberian PA), market (NPM) and democracy (NPG). They distinguish parallel co-existence, transitional combination (ending in replacement), and robust combinations through **layering** (elements sit alongside or on top of one another, like geological sediment) or **blending** (an amalgam whose parts can no longer be told apart). ## Q5. How do theorists judge whether a combined framework makes an original theoretical contribution, as opposed to an application?

### Takeaway
A fused framework counts as a theoretical contribution when it **changes at least one parent theory**, by adding or reconceptualising constructs, relationships, mechanisms or boundary conditions, or by problematizing an assumption. The new insight must also be able to travel beyond the case (analytic generalization, mechanisms portable across contexts). It counts as "application" (Alvesson & Sandberg's "application spotting"; one-way borrowing in Oswick et al.) when the theories are only used to describe a new setting, or the setting is only an illustration. For a mega-event thesis, the test is whether the Games case reveals something about decoupling or public management that ordinary policy settings would not. The case would then be an extreme or revelatory one whose distinctive features (time-bound special-purpose bodies, transnational contractual metagovernance, extreme visibility) are theorised as conditions of the mechanism.

### Cited Findings
- [VC] Whetten (1989): applying a theory in a new context is not enough unless the context changes the theory, for example its boundary conditions. His contribution questions are listed in Q2 — [doi](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1989.4308371)
- [VC] Whetten distinguishes "theories in context" (context as a boundary condition of a general theory) from "theories of context" (context-specific theory). ### Inferences
- [I] An operational **contribution test** the persona can apply, with a pass condition for each item:
  1. **Theory delta**: the thesis can finish the sentence "Before this thesis, decoupling theory or public management theory said X; after it, we know Y". At least one parent theory is changed.
  2. **Mechanism specification**: at least one mechanism (e.g., M1–M6 in Q3) is specified with entities, activities, conditions and observable traces, not just named.
  3. **Boundary conditions**: the conditions under which the mechanism works are stated and are themselves theorised. Examples: time-bound special-purpose authority, external contractual metagovernor, extreme visibility, multi-level fiscal dependency.
  4. **Portability**: implications are drawn for other time-bound, high-visibility, arm's-length delivery settings, such as World Expos, Commonwealth Games, city deals, megaprojects and disaster-recovery authorities (Falleti & Lynch).
  5. **Two-way blending**: the combination returns insights to *both* PA (e.g., accountability design for special-purpose bodies) and institutional theory (e.g., temporal or structural conditions of means–ends decoupling) (Oswick et al.).
  6. **Problematized assumption**: at least one assumption is named and challenged. An example: decoupling theory's implicit assumption of a stable, permanent organization, set against the Games' temporary and sunset organizations; or NPG's assumption that networks spread accountability benignly.
  7. **Artefact**: the contribution is packaged in a recognisable form, such as a process model, typology or propositions (Cornelissen 2017).
- [I] Signs of "application only": the literature review and theory chapter list institutional theory and PA paradigms side by side, but the findings chapter uses them only as labels ("this is NPM", "this is decoupling"); the discussion chapter adds nothing back to the theories; and no boundary conditions are stated.
- [I] Possible original contributions for this thesis, all to be tested:
  - (a) a theory of **temporal means–ends decoupling** in time-bound special-purpose governance (legacy deferral and drift after sunset);
  - (b) a **nested-metagovernance** account of how IOC contractual requirements produce layered legacy talk across government levels;
  - (c) a **typology of legacy-governance arrangements**, crossing structural separation (integrated vs arm's-length) with accountability forum (political, managerial, social) and legacy ownership (assigned vs orphaned);
  - (d) a **process model** of legacy commitments moving through layering, drift and conversion across bid, delivery and post-Games phases.
